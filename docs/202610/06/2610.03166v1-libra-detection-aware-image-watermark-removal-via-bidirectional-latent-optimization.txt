# LiBRA: Detection-Aware Image Watermark Removal via Bidirectional Latent Optimization 

Saibo Ye ♠,†, Huajie Chen ♠,♢,†, Xin Guo †, Le Yang ♢,‡, Chi Liu †, Xiangyu Hu §, Jingjing Guo ¶, and Tianqing Zhu †

Abstract —Digital watermarking supports source attribution for AI-generated images, but its reliability depends on resistance to removal attacks. Some attacks attempt to remove watermarks by forcing the decoded watermark to differ from the original. However, this can produce an inverted watermark that remains detectable, causing removal to fail, while further attempts to alter the watermark may unnecessarily degrade image quality. To address these limitations, we present LiBRA (Latent In-band Bidirectional Removal Attack), which aims to make wa-termarks undetectable while preserving image quality. Instead of continually pushing the watermark toward inversion, LiBRA adjusts the image to conceal the watermark without encouraging further changes that could degrade image quality. Some attacks keep pushing decoded bits away from the original watermark, even when further changes preserve detectability and damage image quality. With access to the watermark key and decoder, LiBRA makes bounded changes in a public autoencoder’s latent space. Unlike inversion-driven objectives that cannot correct excessive inversion, LiBRA guides average decoding confidence toward random guessing from either direction. This helps avoid an inverted but detectable watermark. Leaving individual bits flexible allows image-quality constraints to favor less damaging changes, while an optional frequency-guided mask limits their location. We verify removal using an exact two-sided binomial test rather than assuming the confidence target guarantees success. Experiments on four released watermarking systems show that LiBRA substantially reduces two-sided detection rates while improving image fidelity over the inversion baseline. An iteration study on Stable Signature shows that extending optimization from 

25 to 100 iterations further degrades the inversion baseline’s image quality, while LiBRA maintains stable detection rates and image fidelity. 

Index Terms —Image watermarking, watermark removal, ad-versarial attacks, latent space, two-sided detection.

Alongside watermarking, removal attacks have expanded beyond conventional transformations to exploit decoder feed-back, spectral structures, and generative models. admit fully inverted results. Third, attacks optimize soft confi-dences, whereas detection depends on discrete bit matches, and these quantities can disagree. We examine whether a bidirectional objective on average decoding confidence can counter excessive inversion while preserving image quality during latent-space optimization. Evaluation must examine image-level detection outcomes, since average bit accuracy near chance can conceal removal failures. 

Our Work . We propose LiBRA (Latent In-band Bidirec-tional Removal Attack), a detection-aware watermark removal method operating in latent space. We consider a white-box set-ting in which the adversary can access the watermark decoder and its gradients and knows or can estimate the watermark key. Our goal is to evade a two-sided statistical test while preserv-ing the visual content of the input image. Given a watermarked image, we first encode it using a public, non-watermarked autoencoder, optimize a bounded perturbation in its latent representation, and decode the modified representation into an attacked image. This allows the attack to operate in a compact space without accessing the provider’s watermark-generating components. Latent and image-level fidelity penalties constrain changes to the representation and reconstructed image. An optional frequency-guided mask further restricts perturbations to regions selected from the image content, providing a spatial prior for controlling visual distortion. The central component is a symmetric loss that guides the mean per-bit confidence toward a prescribed near-chance target. When the mean exceeds the target, the loss encour-ages it to decrease; when it falls below the target, the loss encourages it to increase. The objective therefore counteracts excessive inversion while leaving individual bit configurations unspecified. We optimize this loss jointly with the fidelity terms, allowing image preservation to influence how the target is approached. LiBRA uses a single optimization procedure without an outer perturbation-budget search. We evaluate the resulting images with the two-sided test and examine the gap between soft confidence and hard bit accuracy. Comparisons across the evaluated watermarking systems and ablations of supporting components assess whether the objective improves the balance between evasion and fidelity. B. Latent Autoencoding 

A variational autoencoder consists of an encoder E and a decoder D [14]. The encoder maps an image to a lower-dimensional representation, 

L = E(x) ∈ RC×(H/f )×(W/f ),

where C is the latent channel count and f is the spatial downsampling factor. The decoder reconstructs an image from this representation. Latent diffusion models use such represen-tations for image generation [3]. For C = 4 and f = 8 , a 512 × 512 RGB image corresponds to a 4 × 64 × 64 latent representation, reducing the number of optimization variables by a factor of 48 . A differentiable decoder allows gradients computed on the reconstructed image to propagate to the latent representation. However, autoencoder reconstruction is lossy, and small latent changes do not by themselves guarantee small perceptual changes. 

C. Discrete Wavelet Decomposition 

A one-level two-dimensional discrete wavelet transform separates an image into an approximation subband YL and three detail subbands YLH , YHL , and YHH [15]. The approx-imation subband represents coarse image structure, while the 3

detail subbands describe directional changes at a finer scale. Each subband has half the height and width of the input. The Haar transform computes these components using local sums and differences. Its spatial localization allows subband magnitudes to describe where image structure and texture oc-cur. Such information can support a spatial prior, but does not identify where a watermark is embedded. For an orthonormal transform with compatible boundary handling, the sum of squared errors over all subbands equals the squared error in the image domain. 

D. Watermark Detection as a Hypothesis Test 

A watermark decoder produces bit predictions, which the provider converts into a detection decision. Under the unwa-termarked null hypothesis H0, decoded bits are assumed to agree with a fixed key independently with probability 1/2.The random number of matching bits therefore follows 

K ∼ Binomial( N, 1/2) .

For an observed image, the matching count is k = N a (x′).A one-sided test detects unusually high agreement with the key. A two-sided test also detects unusually low agreement, since a nearly inverted key is unlikely under the same null model. Consequently, both near-perfect agreement and near-complete inversion can provide evidence of a watermark [9]. At significance level α, an exact two-sided binomial test determines which matching counts are accepted under the null. The corresponding bit accuracies form the discrete acceptance set AN,α . Here, acceptance means that the test does not detect the watermark; it does not establish that all watermark information has been erased. The exact decision rule used in this work is specified in Subsection IV-A. III. R ELATED WORK 

A. Watermarking for AI-Generated Content 

Classical watermarking embeds signals through spread-spectrum or quantization-based constructions [5], [16]. Learned watermarking changes the embedding mechanism, but retains the need to trade distortion against reliable detection. Watermarking schemes for generative models fall into two families according to where the mark is injected. Endogenous 

schemes couple the watermark to the generation process itself. Stable Signature fine-tunes the VAE decoder of a latent diffusion model so that every image it produces carries a fixed multi-bit signature [7]; GAN fingerprinting embeds a scalable identifier into the generator’s parameters so that outputs remain attributable to a specific model instance [17]; and initial-noise schemes such as Tree-Ring and Gaussian Shading plant a structured pattern in the sampling seed and recover it by DDIM inversion [18] [19], [20]. Exogenous schemes instead treat watermarking as a post-hoc image-processing step, training an encoder–decoder pair to hide and recover a bit string under a differentiable noise layer, as in HiDDeN and its noise-robust successor MBRS [6], [21]. The two families differ in where the watermark is embedded and how much of the pipeline the provider controls. The four systems evaluated in this work expose a common soft per-bit decoding interface, with agree-ment against an N -bit key measured by Equation 1. Our attack therefore does not target a particular embedding mechanism, and we evaluate it on fragile and robust endogenous schemes and on post-hoc ones; broader taxonomies of the design space are available in recent surveys [8]. Beyond these evaluated systems, StegaStamp targets phys-ical capture robustness, while SteganoGAN studies high-capacity learned hiding [22], [23]. RoSteALS uses frozen autoencoder latents for embedding, and TrustMark supports robust watermarking at arbitrary resolution [24], [25]. Water-mark Anything extends decoding to localized messages, and VINE studies robustness against generative image editing [26], [27]. These works broaden the embedding and threat-model landscape; they are related work, not additional victims eval-uated here. 

B. Watermark Removal Attacks 

Watermark removal attacks seek to prevent detection while preserving image content. WEvade optimizes image perturba-tions using watermark-decoder feedback [9]. Its WEvade-W-II variant addresses double-tail detection by targeting a ran-domly sampled watermark and searching over the perturbation budget. UnMarker disrupts spectral structures without detector feedback [10], while regeneration attacks weaken watermarks through learned compression or diffusion-based reconstruc-tion [11], [12]. Lee et al. investigate how information near detection boundaries can support watermark removal [13]. WEvade-W-II is the closest comparison because it explicitly accounts for detectable watermark inversion. LiBRA differs by constraining average decoding confidence rather than pre-scribing a target for every bit. Its symmetric objective corrects deviations on both sides of a near-chance target, while leaving individual bit configurations flexible so that fidelity constraints can favor less damaging changes. LiBRA jointly optimizes watermark and fidelity losses through bounded latent pertur-bations, without the outer perturbation-budget search used by WEvade-W-II. Our matched latent-space comparisons show that this opti-mization procedure reduces runtime while maintaining similar evasion and image fidelity under the tested setting (Sub-section V-F). Beyond this comparison, we evaluate both generation-integrated and post-hoc watermarking systems, quantify the gap between soft-confidence targets and actual detection outcomes, and examine adaptation to an additional detection statistic. The main LiBRA setting assumes knowl-edge or a reliable estimate of the watermark key, whereas WEvade-W-II does not require the true key. 

C. Adaptive Attacks and Evaluation Practice 

Research on adversarial robustness emphasizes evaluat-ing defenses against attacks that account for their mecha-nisms [28]–[30]. For watermark removal, this requires assess-ing success against the provider’s actual detection rule. Low bit accuracy alone is insufficient when inverted watermarks remain detectable. 4

We evaluate image-level detection outcomes and distinguish them from the soft confidence optimized by the attack. In Subsection V-O, we further examine a detector that checks confidence dispersion and an adaptive attack that accounts for this additional statistic. IV. P ROPOSED METHOD

J. Sensitivity to the Defender’s Significance Level 

The exact acceptance set depends on two quantities: the key length N , which the attacker knows, and the significance level 

α, which it does not. Subsection V-A gives the exact accepted count ranges for the evaluated key lengths at α = 0.01 ;here we use YU1 with N = 100 and vary α. Acceptance bounds are computed using exact binomial tests. “Width” is the difference between the largest and smallest accepted bit accuracies. Under the independent fair-bit null, α is an upper bound on the false-positive rate. No re-optimization is required, because the attacked images and their decoded bits are fixed and changing α only moves the threshold of the test applied to them: we re-evaluate the same 5,000 images under detectors that use different α.Table XI shows that decreasing α widens the exact ac-ceptance set. LiBRA has zero observed detections at both 

α = 0 .01 and α = 0 .001 . Increasing α narrows the acceptance set and raises the detection rate to 0.06 at α = 0 .05 and 0.21 

at α = 0 .1. The provider can therefore detect more attacked images by enlarging the rejection region, at the cost of a higher false-positive rate under the stated null model. Here, 

α specifies the test’s nominal significance level and bounds its false-positive rate; the exact rate depends on the discrete binomial distribution.

D ISCUSSION 

A. Evaluation Must Match the Decision Rule 

The same attacked images can count as complete successes under the naive one-sided rule and complete failures under the two-sided test. An inverted decoding retains information about the key, so reporting low bit accuracy alone obscures attribution risk. Evaluation should specify the tested statistic, null model, significance level, and image-level detection rate. Comparing level-calibrated detectors is also necessary when making claims about security at matched false-positive rates; our naive one-sided comparison does not provide that calibra-tion. 14

B. Implications for Watermark Design 

The four evaluated systems share a soft per-bit decoding interface despite different embedding mechanisms. Our objec-tive controls only the mean of those confidences. Dispersion, joint bit patterns, or agreement across disjoint key subsets may expose information that this mean leaves unconstrained. Subsection V-N and Subsection V-O examine dispersion and show why the resulting gain must be assessed against an aware attacker. Redundant keys and joint-pattern tests remain future directions. Multiple rejection rules also require calibration of their combined false-positive rate, for example using a multiple-testing correction [37]; a level of 0.01 per test does not imply the same overall level. 

C. Bidirectional Control and Detection Outcomes 

LiBRA separates the direction encouraged by the optimiza-tion objective from the final detection decision. Proposition 2 shows that per-bit thresholding can still admit fully inverted decodings, whereas Proposition 3 shows that LiBRA reverses its gradient direction when mean confidence crosses the target. This provides a mechanism for counteracting excessive inver-sion while retaining flexibility in individual bit configurations. Reaching the confidence target does not, however, guarantee hard-test acceptance. We therefore evaluate final detection out-comes alongside image fidelity and use controlled comparisons to distinguish the effects of objective design, optimization space, and key knowledge. 

D. Limitations and Threats to Validity 

The attack assumes detector gradients and access to the key or an estimate. It therefore characterizes the stated white-box setting, not an arbitrary black-box deployment. The key-error experiment relaxes one assumption but does not supply missing detector gradients. The target also depends on an as-sumed significance level; different null distributions, statistics, or changing thresholds can require a different objective. The evaluation covers four released checkpoints and their associated data, rather than a broad sweep of training runs. It does not establish transfer to initial-noise schemes such as Tree-Ring and Gaussian Shading [19], [20], whose inversion-based detectors differ from the evaluated interfaces. The spatial prior uses one configuration; interactions between its thresholds and the watermark objective remain unexplored. The reproduced baseline additionally retains a sign-consistency term that LiBRA does not use. The ablations and early-stopping control help separate these factors, but no single comparison establishes universal superiority. Finally, reaching a mean-confidence target neither guarantees hard-count evasion nor minimizes distortion. Per-image outcomes and uncertainty estimates are needed to interpret aggregate means and observed zero detection rates. 

E. Ethical Considerations 

Watermark-removal research can improve robustness eval-uation and can also enable provenance evasion. The study is scoped to publicly released research implementations and controlled evaluation images under explicit capability as-sumptions. The supplied manuscript reports no targeting of 15 

commercial provenance services, private APIs, or deployed infrastructure. Our defensive analysis therefore accompanies the attack, including the adaptive failure of a stronger detector; it should not be read as a claim that adding dispersion alone resolves the vulnerability. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We present LiBRA, a watermark removal method based on bidirectional latent optimization. Its symmetric mean-confidence objective counteracts excessive inversion while allowing fidelity constraints to favor less damaging changes. Experiments across four released watermarking systems show improved evasion and image quality over the inversion base-line. An iteration study on Stable Signature further shows stable evasion and fidelity from 25 to 100 iterations. Our analysis also confirms that reaching the soft target does not guarantee successful removal.
