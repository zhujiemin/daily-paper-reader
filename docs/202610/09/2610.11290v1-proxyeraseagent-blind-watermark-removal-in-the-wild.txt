# ABSTRACT 

Invisible image watermark removal has received growing attention. Despite sub-stantial progress, existing attacks still face a tension between practicality and at-tack specificity. Attacks that exploit detector outputs, decoder responses, or paired watermarked and clean images can be tailored to the watermark decision bound-ary, but they require information that is rarely available in realistic dissemination scenarios. In contrast, attacks based on compression, geometric distortion, or image reconstruction are easy to deploy from a single watermarked image, but they are largely open-loop: they apply generic transformations without knowing whether the current image is moving toward watermark failure. Thus, the key challenge in single-image blind watermark removal is not merely how to trans-form the image, but how to obtain a useful direction for removal without access to the hidden decoder. To bridge this gap, we propose ProxyEraseAgent , an agent-driven framework that recovers attack specificity through proxy decoder responses. Publicly available watermarking schemes provide a natural external knowledge base of candidate decoders, where some are informative for a given unknown watermarked image. Our insight is that a decoder producing a stronger calibrated response to the query image is more likely to share a nearby decoding boundary with the hidden target mechanism. ProxyEraseAgent ranks knowledge-base decoders by calibrated response strength and uses the top ones as proxy boundary estimators. Their responses then guide a progressive search over hetero-geneous removal operations, including geometric distortion, JPEG compression, image reconstruction, and gradient-based perturbation, under perceptual-quality constraints. Experiments across 11 image watermarking systems show that Proxy-EraseAgent achieves an attack success rate of 94.8% , demonstrating the effective-ness of response-guided proxy retrieval and feedback-driven sequential planning for blind watermark removal. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Invisible image watermarking has become an important technique for content provenance, copy-right protection, and synthetic-image identification (Fernandez et al., 2023; An et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2018; Jia et al., 2021). As watermarked images are increasingly disseminated through open platforms, watermark removal attacks have become an important means of evaluating whether wa-termarking systems remain robust under realistic misuse and redistribution (An et al., 2024). In this work, we study a common and practical attack scenario: an attacker observes a disseminated wa-termarked image and aims to make its hidden watermark unrecoverable while preserving the visible content. Unlike settings where the attacker can query a detector, access a decoder, or exploit knowl-edge of the watermarking algorithm, this scenario provides only the image itself. This raises a key question: how can a removal attack remain specific to the hidden watermark when the attacker has access only to the disseminated image? 

The difficulty of this setting does not lie in the absence of possible image transformations. Com-pression, filtering, geometric distortion, reconstruction, and perturbation can all be readily applied to a watermarked image (An et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Saberi et al., 2024; Kassis & Hengart-ner, 2025). The real challenge is that the hidden watermark is not directly accessible: from image 1arXiv:2610.11290v1 [cs.CR] 8 Oct 2026 Figure 1: Comparison of different paradigms for invisible image watermark removal. appearance alone, the attacker cannot tell whether a candidate operation has changed the embedded message, weakened the decoding signal, or moved the image closer to watermark failure. As a result, a visually acceptable transformation may leave the watermark intact, while a stronger transforma-tion may unnecessarily degrade image quality without providing additional disruption. Therefore, the key challenge in single-image blind removal is not merely how to transform the image, but how to build a prior-free yet closed-loop removal process that can adapt its operations according to attack-time signals. Existing watermark removal attacks can be broadly understood from this perspective. Feedback-aware attack rely on target-specific prior knowledge, such as detector outputs, decoder responses, gradients, paired watermarked-clean images, or target-specific surrogate models, to estimate whether a transformation is disrupting the watermark (Jiang et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025). Such information provides a direct estimate of removal progress, but is rarely available when the attacker only observes a disseminated watermarked image. In contrast, transformation-driven attack avoid such assump-tions and applies generic image operations, such as compression, geometric distortion, denoising, regeneration, or detector-free removal (An et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Saberi et al., 2024; Kas-sis & Hengartner, 2025; Shamshad et al., 2026). These attacks do not require target-specific prior knowledge, but they are largely open-loop: they transform the image without estimating whether the hidden watermark has been meaningfully weakened. This reveals a fundamental dilemma: ex-isting attacks either estimate removal progress using unavailable target-specific prior knowledge, or remain prior-free but open-loop. The key to resolving this tension is to recover attack-specific guidance without relying on the hidden target decoder. Ideally, one would identify the exact watermarking algorithm and use its decoder to guide removal, but this is unrealistic when only a single watermarked image is available. We take a different perspective: blind removal does not require identifying the exact watermarking algorithm. Instead, if we can infer from the query image which external decoder behaves similarly to the hidden target decoder, that decoder may provide a useful removal direction. This makes publicly available watermarking schemes valuable. Although most candidates will not match the target mechanism exactly, their responses to the query image can reveal which ones are more likely to approximate the hidden boundary. Based on this idea, we propose ProxyEraseAgent, an agent-driven framework for single-image blind watermark removal. ProxyEraseAgent turns a feedback-free removal problem into a proxy-guided decision process through two coupled stages. First, it performs response-guided proxy decoder re-trieval. Our key insight is that a decoder producing a stronger calibrated response to the query image is more likely to provide an informative proxy for the hidden decoding boundary. Accordingly, Prox-yEraseAgent evaluates candidate decoders from the public watermark knowledge base, calibrates their response strengths using self-encoded references, and selects the top-ranked decoders as proxy boundary estimators. Second, it uses the responses of these proxy decoders to guide removal path planning. Instead of committing to a fixed transformation, ProxyEraseAgent progressively explores a heterogeneous attack space, including geometric distortion, JPEG compression, image reconstruc-tion, and gradient-based perturbation, and selects the attack path that most effectively suppresses proxy responses while satisfying perceptual-quality constraints. In this way, decoder responses play a dual role: they identify informative proxy decoders and provide the feedback signal needed to plan an attack-specific removal path without querying the hidden target decoder. 2Experiments across 11 image watermarking systems show that ProxyEraseAgent achieves an attack success rate of 94.8% , demonstrating that response-guided proxy retrieval and sequential attack planning can provide effective attack-specific guidance without querying the hidden target decoder. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify a gap in blind watermark removal: existing attacks are either prior-dependent or prior-free but open-loop, motivating a proxy-in-the-loop formulation for single-image blind removal. • We propose ProxyEraseAgent , which retrieves informative proxy decoders by calibrated responses and uses their outputs to guide sequential removal planning under perceptual-quality constraints. • We evaluate ProxyEraseAgent on 11 watermarking systems, achieving a 94.8% attack suc-cess rate, with ablations confirming the contributions of proxy decoder retrieval and se-quential attack planning. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Invisible image watermarking. Deep image watermarking has developed from end-to-end encoder–decoder models toward systems tailored to different robustness requirements and deploy-ment scenarios. HiDDeN (Zhu et al., 2018) established a general framework for embedding and de-coding hidden messages in images, while MBRS (Jia et al., 2021), CIN (Ma et al., 2022), FIN (Fang et al., 2023), and PIMoG (Fang et al., 2022) improve robustness against compression, noise, black-box distortions, and screen-capture effects, respectively. Other systems extend watermarking to latent representations, provenance tracking, arbitrary resolutions, and image–video applications, in-cluding RoSteALS (Bui et al., 2023b), InvisMark (Xu et al., 2025), TrustMark (Bui et al., 2023a), VideoSeal (Fernandez et al., 2024), and ChunkySeal (Petrov et al., 2025). This diversity in embed-ding mechanisms, payload capacities, and robustness objectives makes watermark behavior highly heterogeneous, so an attack that is effective for one system may transfer poorly to another. 

Watermark removal. Existing watermark removal attacks can be broadly divided into two paradigms. The first applies predefined image transformations, including geometric transformations and JPEG compression An et al. (2024), denoising or purification Saberi et al. (2024), adversarial perturbations Jiang et al. (2023), and generative reconstruction Zhao et al. (2024). Benchmarking frameworks such as WAVES An et al. (2024) integrate multiple attack families to systematically evaluate watermark robustness, while diffusion- and reconstruction-based methods can effectively disrupt pixel-domain watermarks while preserving much of the visible image content Zhao et al. (2024); Saberi et al. (2024). However, such attacks are typically executed with predefined operators, strengths, or orders, providing little guidance on which operation is most effective for a particular image or target system. As a result, they are difficult to adapt to heterogeneous watermarking mech-anisms on a per-image basis. A second line of work introduces surrogate models to provide attack guidance when the target decoder is unavailable. WEvade optimizes adversarial perturbations on a surrogate detector and transfers them to target watermark detectors Jiang et al. (2023). More recent work further improves transferability by independently training multiple surrogate watermarking models with different architectures or configurations and jointly optimizing attacks across them Hu et al. (2025). Although surrogate-based approaches reduce the need for direct access to the target model, they still rely on additional surrogate-model training, auxiliary data, or a predefined surro-gate set. In contrast, our method does not train dedicated surrogate models from scratch. It treats publicly available watermark decoders as an external knowledge base, retrieves informative proxy decoders according to their calibrated responses to the query image, and uses the resulting proxy feedback to guide adaptive sequential search over heterogeneous attack operations.

3.1 SYSTEM OVERVIEW 

We propose ProxyEraseAgent, a system for single-image blind watermark removal. Given a watermarked image xw, the Response-guided Proxy Retrieval Agent evaluates candidate decoders in the knowledge base and ranks them according to their calibrated responses to the in-put. The top-ranked decoders are selected as proxy decoders and provide feedback for subsequent attack planning. Based on these proxy signals, the Proxy-Guided Attack Planning Agent performs sequential search over a heterogeneous attack space and adaptively composes attack operations to maximize watermark disruption while controlling perceptual distortion. 3.2 KNOWLEDGE BASE 

To obtain external watermark-related feedback without accessing the target decoder, we construct a heterogeneous knowledge base from publicly available image-watermarking systems. We denote the knowledge base as 

K = {(Em, D m, π m)}Mm=1 ,

where Em and Dm denote the encoder and decoder of the m-th watermarking system, respectively, and πm contains the system-specific metadata required for inference, including input preprocessing, image resolution, message length, and decoding configuration. The decoder Dm provides watermark-related responses for proxy retrieval and subsequent attack guidance, while the corresponding encoder Em is used to construct self-watermarked references for response calibration. By incorporating watermarking systems with diverse embedding and decoding mechanisms, the knowledge base provides heterogeneous candidate signals for an unknown target watermark. Given the proxy decoders retrieved in the previous stage, the Proxy-Guided Attack Planning Agent constructs an image-specific watermark-removal path by sequentially searching over a heteroge-neous attack space. Rather than applying a single predefined transformation, the agent evaluates candidate attack states using proxy-decoder feedback and perceptual quality, and progressively re-tains promising attack sequences. 3.4.1 ATTACK ACTION SPACE 

We construct a heterogeneous attack space consisting of four complementary families of watermark-removal operations: geometric distortion, compression distortion, regeneration, and adversarial per-turbation.

These attack families capture different mechanisms for disrupting embedded watermark signals and exhibit complementary effectiveness across watermarking systems. Each family contains multiple attack strengths, allowing the Proxy-Guided Attack Planning Agent to jointly determine the op-eration type and attack strength at each search step. The specific implementations and parameter configurations used in our experiments are provided in Section 4.1. 3.4.2 PROXY -G UIDED SEQUENTIAL ATTACK SEARCH

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets. We evaluate ProxyEraseAgent on 100 randomly selected real-world images from the MS-COCO 2017 dataset (Lin et al., 2014). To evaluate its robustness across heterogeneous wa-termarking mechanisms, each image is independently watermarked by 11 representative image-watermarking systems, including CIN, ChunkySeal, FIN, HiDDeN, InvisMark, LightweightMark, MBRS, PIMoG, RoSteALS, VideoSeal, and TrustMark. We use their publicly released models and official inference configurations. Detailed preprocessing, image resolutions, payload lengths, and decoding settings are provided in Appendix 6.1. 

Attack baselines. We compare ProxyEraseAgent with representative watermark-removal attacks from four categories. Non-geometric baselines include BM3D, brightness and contrast adjustment, Gaussian blur, Gaussian noise, and JPEG compression, while geometric baselines include crop-ping, rotation, and scaling. For regeneration-based attacks, we evaluate Regen-VAE and Regen-Diffusion (Zhao et al., 2024). We further compare with dedicated watermark-removal methods, including AdvEmbB-RN18, AdvEmbB-CLIP (An et al., 2024), and UnMarker (Kassis & Hengart-ner, 2025). For UnMarker, we use the HL configuration following the perceptual-quality constraint in our evaluation. Detailed configurations and parameter ranges are provided in Appendix 6.4.

4.2 MAIN COMPARISON WITH WATERMARK -R EMOVAL BASELINES 

We first compare ProxyEraseAgent with representative watermark-removal baselines, including generic transformations, reconstruction-based attacks, and dedicated watermark-specific attacks. As shown in Table 1, ProxyEraseAgent achieves an overall cASR of 94.8% across 11 watermarking systems, substantially outperforming all baseline methods. The strongest fixed attack, Regen-VAE, achieves an overall cASR of 79.5%, while ProxyEraseAgent improves the success rate by 15.3% under the same perceptual-quality constraint. The performance of fixed attacks varies substantially across different watermarking systems, indi-cating that watermark vulnerabilities are highly mechanism-dependent. For example, Regen-VAE achieves strong performance on several watermarking systems, but its cASR drops to only 3% on RoSteALS and 2% on PIMoG. Similarly, dedicated watermark-specific attacks achieve high effec-tiveness on some systems but exhibit limited transferability to others. In contrast, ProxyEraseAgent achieves the best performance on 9 out of 11 watermarking systems, demonstrating more consistent effectiveness across heterogeneous watermarking mechanisms. These results indicate that a single predefined attack operation is insufficient for blind watermark removal under diverse watermarking schemes. By retrieving informative proxy decoders and adap-tively composing heterogeneous attack operations according to proxy feedback, ProxyEraseAgent can construct image-specific removal paths and achieve stronger attack performance while main-taining perceptual quality. To evaluate the benefit of sequential attack composition, we vary the maximum search depth of Prox-yEraseAgent and analyze the resulting attack performance. A depth of one corresponds to selecting a single attack operation, while larger depths allow the planner to compose multiple complementary operations into an adaptive removal path. As shown in Table 2b, restricting the search depth to one achieves a cASR of 83.91%. Increasing the depth to two substantially improves cASR to 92.72%, while further increasing the depth to three and four achieves 94.45% and 94.82%, respectively. The results demonstrate that the effectiveness of ProxyEraseAgent does not come solely from select-ing an appropriate single attack operation. For many watermark systems, a single transformation is insufficient to achieve effective removal under the perceptual-quality constraint, whereas combining multiple complementary operations provides additional disruption capability. This observation is reflected by the average selected sequence length, which increases from 1.00 at depth one to 2.96 at depth four, indicating that the planner actively utilizes multi-step attack paths when beneficial. Meanwhile, increasing the search depth introduces only a limited additional perceptual cost. The average LPIPS increases from 0.0360 to 0.0421 as depth grows from one to four, while remaining within the predefined quality constraint. Considering the trade-off between attack effectiveness and search complexity, we adopt a maximum search depth of four in the default configuration. 4.5 ROBUSTNESS ACROSS BER T HRESHOLDS

To analyze the effect of self-calibration in proxy decoder retrieval, we compare the distributions of raw response entropy and the proposed self-calibrated scores, as shown in Figure 4. Without calibration, different watermark decoders exhibit substantially different response ranges, making their raw confidence scores difficult to compare directly. # 5 CONCLUSION 

We propose ProxyEraseAgent , an agent-driven framework for single-image blind watermark re-moval without access to the target decoder. ProxyEraseAgent retrieves informative proxy decoders from publicly available watermarking models using calibrated responses, and leverages their feed-9back to adaptively construct effective attack paths. Experiments across 11 watermarking systems show that ProxyEraseAgent achieves an overall cASR of 94.8%, outperforming the strongest fixed attack by 15.3 %. More importantly, our results demonstrate that the key challenge of blind wa-termark removal can be addressed by recovering informative feedback from external watermarking models and using it to guide adaptive, image-specific attack planning. This provides a new perspec-tive for evaluating watermark robustness in realistic settings where the underlying watermarking mechanism is unknown and inaccessible.

Regarding the parameterization of conventional attacks, we follow the settings used in Invis-Mark (Zhao et al., 2024). Specifically, the baseline attack set includes brightness adjustments with factors [2 , 4, 6, 8, 12] , contrast adjustments with factors [2 , 3, 4, 5, 6, 7] , JPEG compression with quality levels [10 , 20 , 30 , 40 , 50 , 60] , additive Gaussian noise with zero mean and standard deviations [5 , 10 , 15 , 20 , 25 , 30] , and Gaussian blur with radii [2 , 4, 6, 8, 10 , 12] . In addition, we evaluate VAE-based compression at quality levels [6 , 5, 4, 3, 2, 1] and diffusion-based perturbations with noise steps [0 , 5, 10 , 20 , 40 , 60 , 80] .For regeneration-based attacks, we evaluate Regen-VAE and Regen-Diffusion proposed by (Zhao et al., 2024).
