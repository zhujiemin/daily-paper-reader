Abstract 

Article 50 (2) of the EU Artificial Intelligence Act requires providers of generative systems to make synthetic outputs machine-readable and detectable, while qualifying the effectiveness, interoperability, robustness, and reliability by technical feasibility, cost, content-specific limits, and the state of the art. For free-form text, one important implementation route is the implementation of a generative watermarking procedure, which poses a compliance problem that is hard to address. Strong watermarking is impossible against adaptive removal, while ordinary edits attenuate statistical evidence, and unmarked human text may overlap distributionally with machine output. This article develops an auditable alternative. First, it defines a description-length robustness profile. A finite-sample bound shows that detectable bias decays and that the required sample size grows with the inverse square of the decay rate. This replaces an unidentified Shannon-entropy constant with collision entropy . Second, it constructs label-conditional conformal prediction sets with separate false-attribution and false-exclusion levels, reporting “watermark supported,” “not supported,” or “inconclusive”. Coverage is obtained as a finite-sample result and is class-conditional under exchangeability. A small reproducible simulation of a tournament watermark confirms both claims and shows that the surviving-token rule overstates the tolerable edit rate roughly twofold. The resulting premarket certificate, signed detector report, and postmarket recalibration protocol operationalize the Commission’s 2026 Code of Practice without claiming universal robustness. 

Keywords: text watermarking, EU AI Act, minimum description length, conformal prediction, synthetic-content provenance, algorithmic accountability 

# 1 Introduction 

Text watermarking is one of the few techniques that can travel with free-form text after metadata is stripped, yet the same editability that makes text useful also makes a statistical watermark fragile. 1arXiv:2610.06317v1 [cs.CR] 5 Oct 2026 The strongest general guarantee, that no efficient adversary can remove a nontrivial mark while preserving output quality, is unavailable under broad, oracle-aided threat models (Zhang et al. 2024). Even without an active adversary, the best possible classifier cannot reliably separate two source distributions that are close in total variation (Sadasivan et al. 2025). At the same time, some evidence shows that generative watermarking can preserve measured quality and provide useful detection on sufficiently long text (Dathathri et al. 2024). The practical question is consequently not whether a watermark is invulnerable, but which evidence survives which transformations, at what error rates, and under which documented conditions. This paper addresses this question under the emerging European regulatory regime. This question became legally concrete on August 2, 2026. Article 50(2) of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 requires providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, image, video, or text to ensure that outputs are machine-readably marked and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated (European Parliament and Council 2024). The provision is not an absolute command. It requires technical solutions to be effective, interoperable, robust, and reliable “as far as” technically feasible, taking account of content limitations, implementation cost, and the generally acknowledged state of the art. The obligation does not apply in the cases that the AI system assists standard editing, without substantially altering the input data provided by the deployer or its semantics, or alternatively, when the AI system is authorized by law to be used to detect, prevent, investigate, or prosecute criminal offences. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 allows for a transition until December 2, 2026 for systems placed on the market before August 2, 2026 (European Parliament and Council 2026). The Commission’s final Code of Practice and Article 50 Guidelines now give the provision an operational setting (European Commission 2026a,b). Article 50(2) requires providers to ensure that their marking and detection solutions are effective, interoperable, robust, and reliable insofar as technically feasible, making performance under relevant uses and transformations part of the compliance inquiry. The Code operationalizes that requirement for signatories through testing and documentation across expected contexts and transformations and, for free-form text, expressly recognizes that detection may sometimes yield comparatively less reliable, robust, or potentially misleading or low-confidence results. In such cases, the evidential limitations of the detector should be made explicit, and the result should not be presented as supporting a categorical conclusion where the available evidence does not justify one.. A single area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) cannot answer the question. It averages across thresholds, obscuring a text’s length and domain, and says little about a document that has moved outside the evaluation distribution. This article proposes a joint technical and governance framework with five contributions: (i) It defines watermark evidence bits , interpreted as a minimum-description-length (MDL) device that yields auditable robustness. (ii) It derives a conservative bound for surviving edits, diluting the observed watermark. (iii) It supplies a two-sided conformal procedure. Separate error levels control false attribution of the target watermark and false exclusion of genuinely marked text. Ambiguous and anomalous cases are reported as inconclusive. (iv) It reports a simulation of a watermark. It recovers the survival law and the corrected frontier to within Monte Carlo error. The surviving-token approximation overstates the tolerable 2substitution rate by a factor of nearly two. (v) It maps these quantities to a certificate, a detector report, and a monitoring loop aligned with Article 50(2)’s criteria of effectiveness, reliability, robustness, and interoperability. The framework delivers less than a universal detector. The outcome is text carrying the watermark of a specified provider, model or sampler version, detector version, and key epoch . Its complement includes human text, output from other models, provider output generated without the target mark, and text from which the mark has been removed. Accordingly, a negative result does not establish human authorship, and a positive result does not establish who submitted, edited, or endorsed the text. Those distinctions are essential since the output may affect a person’s rights or reputation. 

# 2 Regulatory and Technical Background 

Three features of the current legal setting matter for a technical design. First, Article 50(2) states a provider obligation at the system-output layer. It is distinct from the disclosure duties imposed on deployers in other paragraphs. Second, its four performance attributes are expressly contextual and technologically qualified. A technical certificate may therefore delimit supported content types, lengths, languages, transformations, and operating points. Third, compliance is evaluated within a developing ecosystem of standards, codes, and supervisory practice rather than by a single statutory threshold. The regulatory setting was subsequently supplemented by three separate instruments with different legal effects. The Code was the first of these three instruments to reach its final form on 10 June 2026, although the Commission’s work on the Article 50 Guidelines had begun earlier, with a public draft released on 8 May 2026. The Code is a voluntary compliance instrument rather than mandatory legislation. It translates the general requirements of Article 50(2), together with the obligations in Article 50(4) and (5), into more detailed operational commitments concerning matters such as marking techniques, testing, documentation, detector access, and interoperability. On 8 July 2026, the Commission assessed the Code as adequately covering the obligations in Article 50(2), (4), and (5), an assessment followed by the AI Board on 9 July. Article 50(7), as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, expressly provides for the Commission to assess whether adherence to the Code is adequate to ensure compliance with the obligations laid down in paragraphs 2 and 4, while the Code itself continues to address the horizontal requirements of paragraph 5. Adherence does not constitute conclusive evidence of compliance, nor does it give rise to a presumption of conformity of the kind that Article 40 attaches, within its own scope, to compliance with harmonized standards. The Code nevertheless occupies a privileged practical position. The Commission Guidelines describe it as “the only Union-wide recognised practical framework for demonstrating compliance” with the obligations in Article 50(2), (4), and (5). Non-signatories may demonstrate compliance through other adequate means, but those alternatives are not equivalent in practical terms: providers must independently explain and document how their measures satisfy Article 50, may have to carry out a gap analysis comparing those measures with the Code, and may face additional requests for information or access from competent authorities. A second development was Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (the AI Omnibus), adopted by the European Parliament and the Council on 8 July 2026. This instrument is binding legislation and amended the AI Act itself. For present purposes, its principal effect on 3our subject was to introduce a limited transitional rule for Article 50(2). Providers of systems generating synthetic audio, image, video, or text that had already been placed on the market before 2 August 2026 must take the necessary steps to comply with Article 50(2) by 2 December 2026. The four-month transition concerns only this obligation and this category of pre-existing systems; the other transparency obligations in Article 50 remain subject to their ordinary application dates (European Parliament and Council 2026). The third instrument is the European Commission’s Guidelines on the implementation of Article 50, adopted and published on 20 July 2026 under Article 96(1)(d) of the AI Act (European Commission 2026b). The Guidelines are an official interpretive instrument. They explain how the Commission understands the scope and application of the binding obligations contained in Article 50, while leaving the Regulation itself unchanged. For Article 50(2), they clarify, among other matters, the treatment of source code, outputs intended exclusively for machine-to-machine communication, certain outputs used only within closed-loop industrial or product-development environments, standard editing, and narrowly defined business-to-business or industrial contexts. They therefore help identify when the statutory obligation applies without creating an independent marking or detection regime. For the technical framework developed here, the resulting structure is consequential. Article 50(2) supplies the binding legal standard: qualifying outputs must be machine-readably marked and detectable through solutions that satisfy the Regulation’s contextual requirements of effectiveness, interoperability, robustness, and reliability. The Code supplies a more detailed, voluntary implementation route for signatories. The Guidelines are intended to clarify the scope and application of the underlying legal obligation. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 modifies the binding framework itself, including the limited transition for systems already on the market. These instruments perform different functions and should therefore remain analytically separate. This structure also leaves room for uncertainty at the level of individual detection results. Article 50(2) requires a technically adequate marking and detection system under the conditions specified by the Regulation. Conversely, it does not establish that every individual output must permit categorical attribution. The final Code of Practice recognizes that no single marking technique generally satisfies all four Article 50(2) attributes across modalities. It adopts a multilayer approach, combining digitally signed metadata with content that can contain imperceptible watermarking. Free-form text cannot reliably retain container metadata, so the Code treats it as one of two narrow exceptions in which a single marking layer suffices. For free-form text, the Code requires signatories to apply an imperceptible watermark to texts longer than 200 tokens, while recognizing that reliability at such lengths may be lower than for very long texts. At the detection stage, it allows access to free-form-text watermark detectors to be restricted where comparatively lower reliability or robustness may produce misleading or low-confidence results, provided that useful access remains available, in particular to verified expert users with a legitimate need, and that the restriction lasts only until sufficiently reliable and robust detection mechanisms become established. The Code also calls for: • reliability testing across content length, size, diversity, semantics, and expected use contexts, • robustness testing against ordinary processing, including lexical substitution, character insertion or deletion, paraphrasing, and translation cycles, and against adversarial attacks, • interoperable access to detection mechanisms, with watermark-detection interoperability required by February 2, 2027, and 4• downloadable, digitally signed detection results carrying identifying information. These requirements create a need for reproducible claims, bounded scope, auditable error rates, and corrective action. The Commission Guidelines further explain that adherence to the voluntary Code can demonstrate compliance, while a provider using an alternative route must show that its means are adequate (European Commission 2026b). The proposal below involves a measurement layer that can support either route. The Code’s 200-token benchmark deserves particular attention because it links a specific implementation commitment for signatories to a statistical instrument whose evidential power varies with text length. Section 5 shows that, in a controlled simulation, text in the vicinity of that benchmark may still be too short to support a categorical conclusion once ordinary editing is introduced. The point at which the Code requires signatories to apply watermarking should therefore be distinguished from the point at which the resulting evidence becomes sufficiently informative for a categorical attribution. The certificate proposed in this article is designed to make that distinction explicit. AI-text classifiers detect distributional features associated with machine output. Generative watermarks, instead, alter the sampling process using a secret or provider-controlled key and test for the induced statistical pattern (Kirchenbauer et al. 2023). Cryptographic and coding-based work has produced distortion-free, undetectable, and provably robust constructions under different assumptions (Kuditipudi et al. 2024; Christ et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2024; Golowich and Moitra 2024; Qu et al. 2025). These are not interchangeable guarantees. “Undetectable” may mean computational indistinguishability to an observer without the key, whereas “detectable” means recoverable evidence for the authorized detector. “Robust” is meaningful only relative to the properties of the generative process. Each example receives a provenance label Y ∈ { 0, 1} established by controlled generation logs, not inferred by another detector. More general departures yield only approximate bounds, depending on the assumptions adopted (Barber et al. 2023). Every recalibration creates a new detector version and a new calibration hash. 

# 5 A Synthetic Demonstration

On top of that, it adds a fourth result, specifying what happens when a calibration cell is mis-specified. The simulator implements a miniature single-layer tournament watermark. A pseudorandom function with a key maps a packed ( h-token context , token) pair to a value in {0, 1} under a Bernoulli distribution. # 6 An Auditable Article 50 Architecture 

The description-length profile and the conformal set do different jobs. The profile characterizes how evidence degrades across transformations. The conformal set converts a frozen score into set-valued decisions with bounded error for a specified population. Our proposed compliance architecture therefore combines both within a lifecycle. • Proposed predeployment robustness certificate. Before deployment, the provider chooses the generator or sampler, the detector, the tokenizer, the key-epoch, the policy, the target populations, the transformations, and the acceptance criteria. Independent held-out data then produce a certificate that includes negative findings and unsupported cells. Notice that this certificate does not constitute an EU conformity certificate, a conformity assessment, or a presumption of compliance. • Signed per-query detector report. Each detector response identifies the exact system and calibration object used, records both conformal p-values and the prediction set, and cryptograph-ically binds the result to a hash of the tested bytes. It reports whether the item lies within a certified cell and implements the Code’s call for downloadable, signed detection output. • Lifecycle monitoring and recalibration. Providers monitor score distributions, edit channels, detector abuse, false-attribution reports, and model changes. A material generator, tokenizer, detector, or calibration change creates a new version. This lifecycle is consistent with the testing and maintenance approach recommended for synthetic-content transparency (NIST 2024). Table 3 lists the fields a certificate should contain and what each is for. The population row must record the entropy regime as well as the length bin, because Table 2 shows two cells with the same length and edit severity behaving entirely differently when entropy differs. The robustness row must record the edit band used for calibration, because Figure 2b shows that scoring an edited item against an unedited cell inverts the guarantee rather than merely loosening it. The certificate should expose enough aggregate information for scrutiny without publishing secret keys or an attack-enabling transcript. # 7 Discussion and Conclusion 

Text watermarking cannot carry universal artificial content detection. Quality-preserving robustness fails under adaptive threat models. Statistical separability vanishes as source distributions converge. And ordinary editing erases evidence faster than a surviving-token count suggests, by a factor of roughly two in the simulation reported here. None of this makes Article 50 incoherent. The provision itself qualifies its technical attributes by feasibility, content limitations, cost, and the state of the art. The Code adds voluntary implemen-tation commitments concerning layered marking, testing, signed detection results, and continuing improvement, while the Guidelines clarify the scope and application of the statutory obligations. The response is to generate an auditable object. A description-length robustness profile states how much source-specific evidence survives a named transformation. Collision probability and empirical score distributions replace an unidentified entropy multiplier. Token edit distance and semantic loss stay on separate axes. A label-conditional conformal layer then turns the frozen detector score into a supported, unsupported, or inconclusive result, at separately chosen false-attribution and false-exclusion levels, under stated exchangeability conditions. Three findings from the demonstration carry straight into practice. First, a robustness claim built on surviving-token reasoning overstates tolerable editing about twofold, and the overstatement grows with the seed’s window length. Second, a calibration cell that omits the edit band does not degrade gracefully on edited text. It reports “not supported” for nearly every genuinely marked document. Third, assuming a 200-token threshold, editing already leaves most genuinely marked documents inconclusive. A duty to mark is not a warranty of detectability. This architecture does not defeat the impossibility theorem, and it assigns no posterior probability to an individual text. Its value is that it makes claims falsifiable. Regulators gain a way to read “robust and reliable as far as technically feasible” as evidence comparable across providers and over time. Providers gain a way to disclose failure regions without treating every limitation as noncompliance. Watermarking works as an indicator of provenance rather than as an oracle of authorship. 18 A final caveat is that this design has the potential to create an incentives problem for providers, since lower reliability may justify more restrictive access while improvements in detector performance may reduce a provider’s regulatory discretion. The issue is in part endogenous, since the technical limitation that triggers the restriction is itself influenced by the provider’s choices concerning the development, validation, and deployment of the detector.
