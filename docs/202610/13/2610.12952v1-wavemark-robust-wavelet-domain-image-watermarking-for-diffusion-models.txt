ABSTRACT 

Image watermarking enables provenance tracing for photorealistic images generated by diffusion models. However, state-of-the-art (SOTA) removal attacks can suppress embedded signals with lit-tle perceptual degradation. This vulnerability limits the effective-ness of watermarks for intellectual property protection and misuse attribution. We propose WaveMark, a novel wavelet-domain water-marking method that embeds messages in a diffusion model’s initial noise. To support robust recovery from perturbed carriers, Wave-Mark distributes keyed replicas of message bits across spatial po-sitions, wavelet orientations, and scales. Each carrier groups co-efficients from four latent channels and encodes a randomized bit through magnitude projection, providing an explicit decoding mar-gin. We evaluate WaveMark with SD2.1 on SDP and MS-COCO Captions under 14 attacks. WaveMark achieves 100.0% bit accuracy (BA) without attacks, while maintaining image quality comparable to the evaluated baselines. Under SOTA MarkNull and MarkNull-A, it retains over 88% BA, outperforming all baselines. 

Index Terms — AI-generated image watermarking, diffusion model, robust watermark, trustworthy AI, AI security 

1. INTRODUCTION 

The growing accessibility of generative AI (GenAI) has made photo-realistic image synthesis widely available, expanding opportunities for visual content creation while making image provenance harder to verify. AI-generated images can be circulated as photographic ev-idence, facilitating impersonation and misinformation and weaken-ing trust in digital media [1]. Watermarking offers a practical means of addressing this challenge by embedding imperceptible, detectable signals that support the identification and attribution of AI-generated images [2]. Its importance extends beyond detecting synthetic con-tent to enabling source tracing and accountability across the con-tent distribution process. These needs have driven active research into watermarking methods, including approaches that incorporate identifying signals directly into the image generation process while seeking to preserve generation quality [3, 4, 5]. Image watermark-ing is also seeing wider practical use. Google’s SynthID-Image [6], for example, is deployed in Gemini, bringing watermarking into a widely used GenAI service to support more transparent and respon-sible content creation. In-generation watermarking integrates provenance signals into image synthesis rather than applying them after generation. One line of work fine-tunes model components, such as the VAE decoder, so that each generated image carries a recoverable signature [7, 8, 9].

Another line encodes structured patterns or multi-bit messages in the initial noise and recovers them through diffusion inversion [10, 4, 5]. Both approaches enable source detection and, in some cases, user attribution without a separate post-processing step. Embedding the watermark during generation, however, does not make it immune to removal. In addition to compression and geomet-ric distortions, regeneration and adversarial attacks can suppress the watermark through generative reconstruction or optimized perturba-tions while causing little visible degradation [11, 12]. More recent attacks use controllable diffusion-based regeneration [13], manipu-late latent representations through surrogate models [14, 15], or treat the image as an initial video frame and predict its successor [16]. These attacks can remove signals embedded within the generation process itself. Robustness to common distortions is therefore in-sufficient for provenance verification against a deliberate attacker. A practical watermark must remain detectable under such attacks with-out reducing image quality. Advanced removal attacks reduce the reliability of initial-noise watermarks. Regeneration alters the latent signal recovered through inversion, while adversarial perturbations distort the projections used for detection [11, 17, 12, 15]. Both effects can move recovered car-rier projections toward or across the decision boundary. Reliable bit recovery therefore requires a clear carrier-level margin. RQ1: How can we establish a carrier-level decoding margin for reli-able bit recovery? Repeating a bit does not fully solve this prob-lem because replicas exposed to correlated distortions may fail to-gether. This raises RQ2: How should bit replicas be distributed to reduce correlated decoding errors? Increasing the embedding strength widens the separation between bit values, but repeated mod-ifications across carriers can alter the initial-noise energy and reduce image quality. This tradeoff motivates RQ3: How can we control carrier energy while preserving the encoded projection? We ad-dress these questions with the following contributions: • We propose WaveMark, a keyed wavelet-domain watermark-ing framework that distributes randomized bit replicas over disjoint four-channel carriers across spatial positions, wavelet orientations, and scales. • We introduce projection-magnitude embedding, which maps randomized bits to separated projection magnitudes and scales the orthogonal residual to preserve carrier energy in expectation. This design provides an explicit decoding mar-gin for recovering the embedded watermark after attacks. • We evaluate WaveMark under 14 attacks on SD2.1. It achieves 100.0% BA without attacks and retains 88.66% and 88.06% BA under MarkNull and MarkNull-A, respec-tively. We further validate watermark recovery on SD3.5, supporting its applicability across diffusion architectures. arXiv:2610.12952v1 [cs.CR] 9 Oct 2026 2. BACKGROUND Scenarios. We consider user attribution in a text-to-image gener-ation service that supports WaveMark. The provider operates the GenAI service. Each compliant user supplies a personal key and a chosen watermark message, then uses the generated images legiti-mately. During generation, WaveMark uses the supplied key to em-bed the message into the initial noise. We assume an authenticated association between each user account and its key-message pair. At verification, extracting the watermark with the corresponding key and matching it against the registered message supports attribution to the user who requested the image.

4. EXPERIMENTS 4.1. Experimental Setup WaveMark Setup. We generate 512 × 512 images using Stable Diffusion 2.1-base (SD2.1) [21] with 50 DDIM sampling steps and a guidance scale of 7.5. WaveMark embeds a fixed 64-bit message using a 256-bit key. We assign 12 , 12 , 4, and 3 replicas per bit to 

HL 1, LH 1, HL 2, and LH 2 (i.e., R = 31 ), yielding 1,984 disjoint carriers for the 64-bit message. For decoding, we run 50 DDIM inversion steps with an empty prompt and a guidance scale of 1.0. Using Eq. (4), we set ϵ = 10 −3 and use τ = 46 /64 = 71 .875% ,corresponding to an FPR of 3.09 × 10 −4 under H0.

Models and datasets. We use SD2.1 as the default backbone for image generation and for watermarking methods that operate during diffusion generation. Prompts are randomly sampled from the Sta-ble Diffusion Prompts (SDP) dataset [22] and MS-COCO Captions dataset [23]. For each in-generation method, we generate 100 wa-termarked images at a resolution of 512 × 512 using SD2.1. All diffusion generation runs use 50 DDIM denoising steps. 

Watermarking Baselines. We compare our method with six baselines for watermarking AI-generated images, including Stable Signature (SSig) [7], SleeperMark [24], VINE [25], Gaussian Shad-ing (GS) [4], PRC [5], and T2SMark [3]. 

Attack baselines. We evaluate WaveMark against five common image distortions: Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur, 50% random crop-ping, contrast adjustment, and JPEG compression. We also consider regeneration-based removal attacks, including DA [11], VA [17], CtrlRegen+ [13], and NFPA [16], as well as adversarial attacks, in-cluding UnMarker [12], Imprint [14], and NPA [26]. We addition-ally evaluate robustness against two recent SOTA removal attacks: MarkNull and MarkNull-A [15]. We follow the attack configura-tions reported in the original papers and apply each attack to 100 generated watermarked images per watermarking method.

WaveMark has the highest CLIP-IQA on SDP and ranks second on MS-COCO Cap-tions, which suggests that embedding the watermark has little effect on perceived image quality. Its CLIP-Score decreases from 27.648 on SDP to 25.680 on MS-COCO Captions. Robustness Evaluation. Table 2 compares WaveMark with six baselines under 14 attacks. WaveMark achieves 98.84% to 100.0% BA under common distortions, including 99.75% under 50% random cropping with black padding and 99.81% under contrast adjustment. Under regeneration attacks, BA reaches 98.25% for DA and 96.72% for VA, but decreases to 79.47% for CtrlRegen+ and 54.18% for NFPA. Under the SOTA MarkNull and MarkNull-A attacks, Wave-Mark achieves the highest BA of 88.66% and 88.06%, respectively, improving on the strongest baselines by 27.30% and 13.32%. NFPA treats the watermarked image as an initial video frame and predicts the next frame to remove the watermark. Although this suppresses the watermark, the resulting images differ substantially from the original inputs [15, 16].Except for NFPA, WaveMark’s BA remains above the 71.875% detection threshold under all evaluated attacks. These results demonstrate strong robustness to common distortions and adversarial removal attacks, providing a practical foundation for reliable watermark detection and user attribution. 

Cross-architecture Compatibility. To assess compatibility across diffusion architectures, we evaluate WaveMark on Stable Diffusion 3.5-Medium (SD3.5) [30], extending our experiments from the U-Net-based SD2.1 to the DiT architecture. WaveMark achieves 99.81% and 100.0% BA on SDP and MS-COCO Captions. The corresponding CLIP scores are 28.963 and 26.513, CLIP-IQA scores are 0.937 and 0.922, and FID values are 81.079 and 205.881. These results demonstrate reliable watermark recovery and detection on SD3.5, supporting WaveMark’s compatibility with both U-Net and DiT architectures. 

5. CONCLUSION 

We have proposed WaveMark, a robust wavelet-domain watermark-ing method that embeds messages in the initial noise of LDMs. It distributes keyed message replicas across wavelet subbands and en-codes each replica in a carrier’s projection magnitude to improve the robustness against advanced watermark removal attacks. On SD2.1, WaveMark achieves 100.0% BA without attacks and retains 88% BA under MarkNull and MarkNull-A, outperforming all baselines. Ex-periments on SD3.5 also support its applicability to a DiT architec-ture for watermark embedding.
