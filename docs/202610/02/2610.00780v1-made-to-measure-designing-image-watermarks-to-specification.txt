ABSTRACT
Image watermarking supports provenance and attribution by embedding verifiable
identity information into images. Practical deployments, however, must jointly
satisfy requirements for attack resistance, false-positive rate (FPR), image qual-
ity, and latency. Existing watermarking methods are robust to different classes
of transformations, so combining complementary methods can provide broader
protection than any single watermark. Such composition is challenging, as addi-
tional fragments increase distortion and decoding cost and must share the same
FPR budget. Therefore, we propose TAILOR, a request-conditioned watermark
composition framework with three stages: (1) offline characterization measures
fragment recovery, distortion, and runtime as response curves over embedding
strength; (2) joint configuration selection encodes the request as an SMT model
over these curves and solves for the lowest-distortion composition of fragments,
strengths, order, and geometric recovery; and (3) live calibration validates the se-
lected configuration on the user’s images and refines predictions that fail to trans-
fer. Experimental results across 7,321 distinct requests spanning five scenarios and
20 attack settings show that TAILOR achieves 96.21% scenario-averaged request
satisfaction with a mean PSNR of 41.02 dB, outperforming existing methods in
robustness while achieving consistently better image quality. Code is available at
https://github.com/aaFrostnova/Tailor.

INTRODUCTION
The growing use of advanced generative image models such as GPT-Image-2 (OpenAI, 2026), and
Nano Banana 2 (Google DeepMind, 2026) has made provenance and attribution important con-
cerns for creators and service providers. Invisible image watermarking associates an image with a
machine-readable signal that can be checked after distribution (Bui et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2025), an
approach that has already seen commercial deployment and demonstrated significant practical value.
For example, Google introduced SynthID for Imagen customers on Vertex AI to help identify AI-
generated images (Gowal & Kohli, 2023), while Meta introduced invisible watermarking for images
generated by Meta AI to improve transparency and traceability (Meta, 2023). These deployments
demonstrate the practical role of watermarking in provenance and attribution.
Existing watermarking methods improve robustness through different designs. TrustMark learns to
balance image quality and watermark recovery under image perturbations (Bui et al., 2023). VINE
leverages generative priors and surrogate distortions to improve robustness against image editing (Lu
et al., 2025), while VideoSeal targets efficient watermarking with robustness to geometric transfor-
mations and video compression (Fernandez et al., 2024). Their designs therefore provide comple-
mentary protection against different transformations, while selecting a single watermark or tuning
its strength cannot exploit the protection offered by the others. Combining multiple watermark
mechanisms is thus attractive for achieving broader robustness.
Practical deployment, however, requires more than robustness alone. A watermark configuration
must survive expected transformations while preserving image quality, satisfying a false-positive-
rate (FPR) requirement, and meeting runtime constraints. For example, SynthID is designed to re-
main detectable after common image modifications while preserving image quality (Gowal & Kohli,
2023), and the SynthID-Image report further highlights low encoding overhead, high-throughput

the relevant attack surface (Gowal et al., 2025). When multiple watermarks are combined, their
fragments must share the same quality, latency, and FPR budgets, while their embedding strengths
and order jointly affect robustness and distortion. Moreover, offline measurements may not transfer
directly to the user’s images. These coupled requirements motivate treating the expected attacks,
FPR budget, image-quality floor, and runtime limit as deployment-specific inputs. The challenge is
therefore to jointly select and validate a watermark composition that satisfies the complete request.
To address these challenges, we propose TAILOR, a request-conditioned framework for compos-
ing and validating image watermarks (Figure 1). The core insight is to treat existing watermark
encoder and decoder pairs as complementary fragments whose configurations should be optimized
jointly rather than selected independently. TAILOR first characterizes fragment recovery, distor-
tion, runtime, and geometric recovery offline and represents these measurements as continuous re-
sponse models. Given a request specifying attacks, FPR, image quality, and latency, an SMT-based
solver (Barrett & Tinelli, 2018) jointly selects the fragments, embedding strengths, order, and geo-
metric recovery stages that satisfy the complete specification while minimizing predicted distortion.
Live calibration then evaluates candidate compositions on the user’s images and refines inaccu-
rate offline predictions when necessary. We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of TAILOR on
7,321 distinct deployment requests spanning five scenarios and 20 attack settings. TAILOR achieves
96.21% scenario-averaged request satisfaction with a mean PSNR of 41.02 dB, outperforming ex-
isting methods in robustness while achieving consistently better image quality (Table 1). Controlled
ablations further validate the contributions of candidate search, watermark composition, and strength
adaptation. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We formulate request-conditioned watermark deployment and introduce TAILOR. Given
an attack set, an FPR budget, a quality floor, and a latency ceiling, the task is to construct
a watermark configuration satisfying the complete specification rather than select a fixed
watermark in isolation.
• We develop a configuration solver that integrates measured performance models with live
calibration. SMT jointly optimizes fragment choices and settings under the requested qual-
ity and latency budgets and configuration-dependent verification thresholds, returning ei-
ther a valid configuration or reporting that no configuration found within the encoded model
and the candidate budget.
• We conduct extensive evaluations on 7,321 deployment requests spanning five scenarios
and 20 attack settings. TAILOR achieves 96.21% scenario-averaged request satisfaction,

RELATED WORK
Image Watermarking and Attacks.
Post-hoc watermarking uses transform-domain methods
based on DWT with DCT (Al-Haj, 2007) and DWT with DCT and SVD (Navas et al., 2008), or
learned encoder and decoder models such as RivaGAN (Zhang et al., 2019), StegaStamp (Tancik
et al., 2020), WAM (Sander et al., 2025), TrustMark (Bui et al., 2023), and VINE (Lu et al., 2025).
These methods face signal-processing attacks such as JPEG compression, blur, and noise; geometric
attacks such as cropping, resizing, and rotation (An et al., 2024); adaptive and optimization-based re-
moval attacks such as WEvade (Jiang et al., 2023) and UnMarker (Kassis & Hengartner, 2025); and
regeneration attacks such as Regen (Zhao et al., 2024b) and CtrlRegen+ (Liu et al., 2025). Regen-
eration attacks reconstruct perceptually similar images while suppressing watermark signals (Zhao
et al., 2024b; Liu et al., 2025). RAVEN (Shamshad et al., 2026) further exploits diffusion-based
novel-view synthesis for watermark removal. Different watermarks are effective against different
attacks, while no single method is uniformly robust across the evaluated threat landscape (An et al.,
2024). In our evaluation, VINE better withstands regeneration but is vulnerable to the tested ge-
ometric attacks, whereas TrustMark shows the opposite pattern. This complementarity motivates
combining watermarks, although sequential embedding can introduce interference and image dis-
tortion, and additional decoder executions increase latency.
Scenario-Dependent Watermark Configuration.
Practical deployments impose requirements
beyond average watermark recovery. Adobe Content Authenticity combines invisible watermark-
ing with Content Credentials and digital fingerprinting for provenance across transformations such
as metadata removal and screenshots (Parsons, 2024; Adobe, 2025), while SynthID emphasizes
image-quality preservation, robustness to common edits, and efficient detection (Gowal & Kohli,
2023; Gowal et al., 2025). OmniGuard (Zhang et al., 2025) similarly combines copyright protection
with manipulation localization, illustrating that watermarking objectives vary by application. FLEX-
Mark (Nicholas et al., 2026) further argues for evaluation under shared distortion budgets, realistic
payloads, low false-positive operating points, and adaptive removal rather than average recovery
alone. These works motivate configuration at the system level: a social-media deployment may
prioritize crop and compression robustness, while another may prioritize regeneration resistance, at-
tribution reliability, payload capacity, image quality, or latency. Existing work develops stronger in-
dividual watermarks, broader attack benchmarks, fixed multi-purpose architectures, and watermark
ensembles (Petrov et al., 2026), but to our knowledge does not directly treat deployment as a per-
request constrained optimization over heterogeneous watermark fragments, their order, strengths,
and recovery mechanisms. We therefore propose TAILOR, a request-conditioned framework that
jointly selects watermark fragments, embedding order, strengths, and geometric recovery support
under user-specified robustness, false-positive, image-quality, and latency constraints.

METHODOLOGY
In this section, we introduce TAILOR, a request-conditioned watermark composition framework.
Given a deployment request u = (Au, α, q, t), TAILOR constructs a watermark configuration that
withstands every attack in Au at false-positive rate α, with image quality at least q and latency at
most t. It proceeds in three stages. First, Offline Characterization profiles each watermark frag-
ment and geometric recovery stage and stores the results in a performance database. Second, Joint
Configuration Selection encodes the request as an SMT model over this database and solves for
the lowest-distortion configuration. Third, Live Calibration validates the resulting candidate on the
user’s images under the requested attacks before deployment. Figure 2 provides an overview of
TAILOR.
4.1
OFFLINE CHARACTERIZATION
Measurements.
Offline characterization builds the Performance Database used to predict candi-
date configurations before live calibration. For each fragment f ∈{VINE, TrustMark, VideoSeal},
we sweep its native embedding strength λf and measure, under every attack a, the mean bit accuracy
bf(λf, a) together with the underlying per-image scores. We also record standalone embedding dis-
tortion Df(λf) as mean squared error (MSE), and embedding and decoding latency. To characterize
composition, δg→f(λg, a) measures the recovery loss of f when g is embedded after it, while efg
measures excess pairwise distortion beyond the standalone terms. We repeat the relevant measure-
ments for the four optional geometric recovery stages derived from the fragments’ geometric failure
modes: scale, tile, resynchronization, and angle search (Appendix B). All offline measurements use
a fixed image subset disjoint from the images later used for live calibration (Appendix C.1).

4.2
JOINT CONFIGURATION SELECTION
Given a deployment request u = (Au, α, q, t), TAILOR must jointly decide which watermark frag-
ments to use, how strongly and in what order to embed them, and whether to enable geometric
recovery. These choices are coupled: adding a fragment may improve attack coverage but also in-
crease distortion, latency, and the number of verification paths sharing the FPR budget. We therefore
formulate configuration selection as a joint constrained optimization problem.

Request-Local Refinement.
Candidates are tested in order of predicted distortion, up to three
distinct discrete structures. If all fail, the measured fragment accuracies and pass rates replace the
corresponding predictions for this request, and the solver proposes one revised candidate, which
must pass the same live check. If no candidate passes under the current screening margins, TAILOR
retries with the smaller margins of a predeclared schedule, keeping the request, the FPR accounting,
and the live criteria unchanged (Appendix A.5).
Outcome.
The first candidate that passes every live check is returned as cout, and the outcome is
SAT. The deployment then watermarks its images with Ecout and verifies them with Dcout at the
same threshold τ(cout, α). If no candidate passes, the outcome is UNSAT: no configuration found
within the encoded model and the candidate budget meets the request on the user’s images.

EVALUATION
5.1
EXPERIMENT SETUP
Datasets and Requests. We assemble a pool of natural and generated images from MS-COCO (Lin
et al., 2014), DiffusionDB (Wang et al., 2023), UltraEdit (Zhao et al., 2024a), DIV2K (Agustsson
& Timofte, 2017), and LSDIR (Li et al., 2023), together with images generated by DALL·E 3 and
SDXL. These sources cover diverse image characteristics, including real photographs, diffusion-
generated content, editing-oriented images and multiple generative models, providing broad cover-
age of realistic deployment inputs. Each deployment request specifies an attack set, an FPR budget,
an image-quality floor, and a latency ceiling. After sampling and deduplication, the benchmark
contains 7,321 distinct requests over 20 attack settings across five scenarios: signal processing and
re-encoding (S1), geometric transformations (S2), regeneration and editing (S3), watermark removal
(S4), and broad attack coverage (S5). Figure 3 shows how many requests in each scenario include
each attack; a request may include multiple attacks. Image sampling and attack settings are detailed
in Appendices C.1 and C.2.
Baselines. We compare TAILOR with MaskWM (Hu et al., 2026), StegaStamp (Tancik et al.,
2020), VINE (Lu et al., 2025), TrustMark (Bui et al., 2023), and VideoSeal (Fernandez et al., 2024)
at unit embedding strength. For VINE and TrustMark, we additionally evaluate variants equipped
with geometric recovery stages (Geo.). We also consider two composition baselines. TAILOR-
G incrementally adds the fragment with the largest predicted coverage gain without backtracking,
while reoptimizing strength, embedding order, and geometric recovery stage for each candidate
subset using the same offline database and SMT model as TAILOR. Enumeration evaluates all seven
nonempty watermark subsets at unit strength and a fixed embedding order, while Enumeration +
Geo. additionally evaluates geometric recovery stages. All methods use the same requests, live
images, and acceptance gates. Detailed baseline policies are provided in Appendix C.3.

5.2
EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
Request Satisfaction. We evaluate whether TAILOR can satisfy complete deployment requests
across different attack scenarios. As shown in Table 1, TAILOR achieves scenario-averaged satis-
faction of 96.21%, compared with 91.05% for the greedy variant and 83.21% for Enumeration +
Geo. The gap over greedy shows that configuration search remains important even when both meth-
ods share the same offline model, while the gap over Enumeration + Geo. shows that enumerating
unit-strength compositions does not replace joint optimization of fragments and their parameters.
TAILOR satisfies all S1 and S2 requests, reaches 97.31% on S3 and 98.34% on S5, and achieves
85.40% on the more challenging S4. Across all attack-conditioned request groups in Figure 5, TAI-
LOR also achieves the highest satisfaction among the compared methods, demonstrating consistently
strong coverage across the attack suite.

TAILOR consistently provides better image fidelity than the baselines.

5.3
ABLATION STUDIES
Candidate Search and Live Refinement. We evaluate whether broader candidate search and live
feedback improve request satisfaction. As shown in Table 2, SMT-top1 satisfies 80.65% of re-
quests, while evaluating up to three candidate structures increases satisfaction to 91.99%, a gain of
11.34 percentage points. Request-local refinement and margin fallback further raise satisfaction to
95.50%, adding another 3.51 points. This shows that the highest-ranked offline candidate is not al-
ways sufficient and that broader search and live refinement recover additional feasible deployments.
On jointly accepted requests, SMT-top1 and SMT-top3 select the same configurations as TAILOR,
yielding identical mean PSNRs of 41.22 and 40.83 dB on their respective intersections. Thus, the
additional search expands coverage without degrading fidelity on already successful requests.
Watermark Composition. We evaluate whether optimizing a single watermark per request can
replace composition. Single-fragment retains strength optimization, geometric recovery stage selec-
tion, candidate search, and the full live-validation procedure, but satisfies only 63.64% of requests,
compared with 95.50% for TAILOR. The 31.86-percentage-point gap shows that a single optimized
watermark is insufficient for requests involving heterogeneous attacks. On jointly accepted requests,
TAILOR achieves 42.20 dB compared with 41.84 dB for Single-fragment, showing that composition
improves both coverage and fidelity.
Strength Adaptation. We evaluate the contribution of per-request strength optimization by fixing
all selected watermark strengths to one. Unit-strength retains subset, order, geometric recovery
stage, candidate search, and live refinement, but satisfies only 73.18% of requests, compared with
95.50% for TAILOR. On jointly accepted requests, TAILOR achieves 41.40 dB versus 37.92 dB for
Unit-strength, a 3.48 dB gain. These results show that strength adaptation is important for jointly
improving robustness and image fidelity. Additional ablation studies are provided in Appendix D.

CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose TAILOR, a framework for composing image watermarks to meet
deployment-specific requirements. TAILOR combines SMT-based joint configuration selection with
live calibration to choose complementary watermark fragments and embedding settings under spec-
ified attacks, FPR budgets, quality floors, and latency budgets. Experiments on 7,321 distinct re-
quests across five scenarios demonstrate higher request satisfaction than fixed single-watermark and
shared-model greedy baselines, together with improved image quality on jointly accepted requests.
This work provides a basis for adapting watermark composition to the provenance and attribution
needs of different applications.

SMT Formulation.
Configuration selection couples discrete structural choices with continuous
embedding strengths. Fragment selection, embedding order, and geometric recovery are discrete
decisions, whereas each selected fragment has a continuous native strength; these variables interact
because predicted recovery and distortion depend on the complete composition. SMT (Barrett &
Tinelli, 2018) is therefore well suited to the problem, since it combines Boolean structure with
arithmetic constraints in a single model.
We encode all request requirements and configuration-validity conditions as an SMT formula over
Boolean variables and linear real arithmetic. A satisfying assignment directly specifies a feasible
configuration, while an UNSAT result indicates that no configuration satisfies the encoded require-
ments within the characterized search space. We use Z3 (de Moura & Bjørner, 2008) to solve the
resulting model.
Optimization is performed by repeated satisfiability queries. After obtaining a feasible configuration
with objective value Dbest, we add the constraint that the next solution must improve distortion by
at least ϵ and solve again. This process continues until no improving assignment exists, yielding the
lowest-distortion configuration up to the specified tolerance.

correction only when the predicted rate exceeds the observed rate by more than two standard errors.
When mean-recovery and acceptance-rate evidence produce corrections to the same cell, we retain
the more conservative one.
16


Request-Local Model Update.
Each accepted correction shifts the corresponding response curve
rather than only the measured strength. A pointwise update could allow the next solve to move to a
neighboring strength on the same optimistic curve and reproduce the failed configuration. Shifting
the curve preserves its measured shape while correcting its request-specific level; the associated per-
image scores are shifted consistently so that mean and acceptance-rate predictions remain aligned.
If the failed candidate used a geometric recovery stage, the correction is applied to that stage’s
response curve, since that is the prediction used by the solver.
All corrections are applied to a request-local copy of the Performance Database and never modify
the shared offline measurements. TAILOR then re-solves the joint configuration problem once using
the corrected model, and the resulting candidate must pass the same live validation criteria before it
can be accepted.
A.6
WATERMARK DEPLOYMENT
The composition library contains VINE (Lu et al., 2025), TrustMark (Bui et al., 2023), and
VideoSeal (Fernandez et al., 2024). Their native controls are VINE residual scale, TrustMark em-
bedding strength, and VideoSeal blend scale. Every fragment transmits the same 100-bit word:
VINE and TrustMark carry 100 bits natively, whereas VideoSeal’s message vector is longer, so the
deployment writes the word into its first 100 positions and reads back the corresponding 100 logits,
leaving the rest unused. Sequential embedding in the accepted order follows Eq. 4, with w entering
each fragment through that fragment’s own key-derived permutation and mask.
At verification, the decoder reverses each fragment’s key-derived permutation and mask, evaluates
the permitted individual readouts and primary fusion, and then applies the enabled geometric search
when needed. All paths use the same configuration-dependent threshold in Eq. 14.

B.2
FAILURE-DRIVEN GEOMETRIC RECOVERY
Scale, for a payload on one radius.
A ring occupies a single radius, so a centred crop of side
factor s moves a ring embedded at radius K to K/s and cuts off every ring with K > s. Embedding
the same codeword at several radii therefore leaves one ring inside the frame for any crop down to
the smallest radius, and recovery reduces to finding which one: the decoder reads VINE at a dense
grid of centred scales and keeps the first view that verifies. We embed at K ∈{1.00, 0.75, 0.50}.
Each radius covers a different crop depth. Under a crop to half the area only the innermost ring is
still inside the frame, and it is read at scale 0.50/
√
0.5 ≈0.71; the outer two are gone. The smallest
radius is 0.50 because VINE encodes at 256×256 on a 512×512 canvas, so that ring is the smallest
one that needs no resampling. The decoder reads VINE at 133 centred scales from 0.34 to 1.00 in
steps of 0.005. The step is 0.005 because a ring only reads within roughly 1% of its own scale,
which leaves no gradient for a coarse-to-fine search to follow, and the range starts at 0.34 because
no smaller view can hold the innermost ring.
Tile, for a payload on one grid.
A spread codeword survives a centred crop that keeps enough
area, but it is still written on one fixed grid, so a translation moves every position off that grid at
once. Repeating the whole codeword in each cell of a 2 × 2 grid removes the dependence on where
the frame starts: any window of one cell size that lands on a cell carries a complete codeword. The
decoder slides a 256 px window over the frame at a stride of 32 px, 81 positions in all, and verifies
each one, so recovery needs a surviving cell rather than a surviving frame.
Resync, for a warped frame.
A rotation or a homography does not remove the payload; it moves
it away from where every decoder looks, which is why all three fragments fail together under it.
One estimate of the frame therefore repairs all of them at once, and it is worth spending embedding
capacity on making that estimate possible: the stage appends a SyncSeal template (Fernandez et al.,
2026) whose predicted corners give the homography back to the original frame. The decoder rectifies
once and reads every selected fragment from the result, then repeats at tilts of 3 and 6 degrees either
way to absorb the residual error of the corner prediction, five views per fragment in all.
Angle, for rotation without a template.
Rotation leaves evidence in the image itself: an in-place
rotation pads the corners with fill, and the boundary between fill and content is a straight line whose
slope is the rotation. Reading the angle from the frame rather than from a fragment costs nothing at
embedding and keeps the estimate independent of which fragments a request selected, so a rotation-
fragile choice of fragments cannot disable it. The decoder proposes the angles implied by the corner
wedges first, then falls back to a fixed sweep on a 10 degree grid so that the search stays complete
when no wedges are present, at most 65 views per fragment.
Scope.
Scale and tile change how one fragment is written, so each applies only to that fragment and
only that fragment is read from their views. Resync and angle restore the frame, so every selected
fragment is read from each of their views. This is the origin of the per-stage test counts in Eq. 12: the
two frame-restoring stages scale with the number of selected fragments, the two fragment-specific
stages do not.

C.1
IMAGE POOL AND MEASUREMENT ROLES
Images are drawn from five source groups:
• MS-COCO 2017 (Lin et al., 2014): real photographs with diverse native resolutions.
• DiffusionDB (Wang et al., 2023): SD-1.5 generated images at 512 × 512.
• UltraEdit (Zhao et al., 2024a): real images and image-editing examples at 512 × 512.
• DALL·E 3 / SDXL (Podell et al., 2024): generated images at 1024 × 1024.
• DIV2K (Agustsson & Timofte, 2017) and LSDIR (Li et al., 2023): high-resolution real
images ranging from 1024 pixels to approximately 2K resolution.
All experiments use three fixed, mutually disjoint slices of 100 images, each containing 25, 25, 25,
15, and 10 images from the five source groups above. Two slices are used exclusively for offline
19


characterization: one for fragment strength sweeps, pairwise interaction measurements, distortion
modeling, and geometric recovery stages, and the other for regeneration, rinse, CtrlRegen+, and
watermark-removal attacks. The third slice serves as the live set for request-local calibration and all
reported evaluations, and is shared across requests. File-, byte-, and pixel-level digest checks con-
firm that the three slices contain no overlapping images, ensuring strict separation between offline
characterization and live evaluation.
C.2

We construct deployment requests over attacks with different adversarial capabilities. In all settings,

of publicly available pretrained models; and Optimization-Based Removal, which allows iterative
optimization directly on the released image.
The watermark-removal scenario (S4) includes CtrlRegen+ at strength 0.3 or 0.5, optionally com-
bined with UnMarker. Both target watermark removal but use different mechanisms: CtrlRegen+

ADDITIONAL ABLATION STUDIES
We examine the screening margin, fragment interference, recovery-interaction model, and strength
discretization.
D.1
IMPACT OF THE SCREENING MARGIN
We evaluate how the initial rate-screening margin affects the trade-off between request coverage
and image fidelity. Using the same 7,321 requests and fixed live-validation criteria, we compare
m0 = 0.04 and m0 = 0.06. As shown in Table 8, increasing the margin raises TAILOR’s scenario-
averaged satisfaction from 95.58% to 96.21%, while mean PSNR over accepted requests decreases
from 41.61 to 41.02 dB.
The coverage gain is concentrated in S4, where satisfaction increases from 82.24% to 85.40%, cor-
responding to 26 additional accepted requests among its 822 requests; the other four scenario rates
remain unchanged. The greedy variant is more sensitive to the same change, increasing from 82.75%
to 91.05% satisfaction while its mean PSNR decreases from 41.77 to 40.91 dB. These results show
that a stronger screening margin can steer the search toward configurations that satisfy more diffi-
cult requests at some fidelity cost, while the full search remains comparatively stable across the two
settings.
D.2
CONTINUOUS VS. GRID STRENGTHS
We evaluate whether continuous strength optimization provides an advantage over selecting only
the strengths explicitly measured during offline characterization. The grid variant restricts each
fragment to the frozen response-curve knots while preserving all other configuration choices and the
same live-validation procedure. Table 9 compares the two variants on the 1,061 requests accepted by
both. Continuous optimization improves mean PSNR by 0.36 dB on this matched set, showing that
interpolation between measured strengths enables TAILOR to find lower-distortion configurations
than discrete strength selection alone.

zero scores counted as errors. Each mean is computed over all 100 images without filtering. We
use the same 64-of-100 verification threshold for every configuration so that threshold differences
cannot explain changes in recovery.
Standalone mean bit accuracy is 99.97% for VINE, 100.00% for TrustMark, and 99.59% for
VideoSeal. Across all ordered pairs and triples, the corresponding composed readouts average
99.37%, compared with 99.85% for their standalone counterparts, a decrease of only 0.49 percentage
points. The largest mean loss is 0.02 points for VINE and 0.04 points for TrustMark. VideoSeal is
more order-sensitive: its loss remains below 0.08 points when embedded last in a triple but can reach
4.05 points under other orders. Nevertheless, every selected fragment remains above the verification
threshold across all 100 images for every tested composition.
These results show that the selected fragments can coexist with low verification-level interference,
while embedding order remains important, particularly for VideoSeal. Together with their com-
plementary robustness across attack scenarios, this supports VINE, TrustMark, and VideoSeal as
compatible building blocks for the fragment library of TAILOR.
D.4
RECOVERY-INTERACTION SENSITIVITY
We further test how sensitive TAILOR is to explicit modeling of recovery interactions between com-
posed fragments. The NoRecoveryInteraction variant removes pairwise recovery corrections and
composition-conditioned stage adjustments from the surrogate, while retaining live validation, local
refinement, and margin fallback. At m0 = 0.04, it satisfies 1,060 of the 1,111 requests, compared
with 1,061 for the full method (95.41% versus 95.50%). On the 1,060 jointly accepted requests,
mean PSNR is 40.690 dB for the variant and 40.664 dB for the full method, with a median paired
difference of zero. The negligible change in both satisfaction and fidelity shows that the complete
pipeline is largely insensitive to explicit recovery-interaction modeling, consistent with the low in-
terference observed in direct superposition. Together with their complementary robustness, this sup-
ports VINE, TrustMark, and VideoSeal as sufficiently compatible building blocks for the fragment
library of TAILOR.

LIMITATIONS
TAILOR relies on offline characterization and can only optimize over watermarks, attacks, and recov-
ery mechanisms represented in its performance database. Supporting new components or previously
unseen attacks therefore requires additional profiling and database updates before they can be incor-
porated into configuration selection. In addition, the search is limited by the coverage and accuracy
of the offline surrogate model; feasible configurations that are poorly represented by the database
may be missed before live validation. Live calibration helps address prediction errors by providing
measurement-backed corrections when offline predictions do not transfer accurately to the target
deployment.
