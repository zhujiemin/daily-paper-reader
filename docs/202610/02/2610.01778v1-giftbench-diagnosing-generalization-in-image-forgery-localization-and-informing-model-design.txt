GIFTBench: Diagnosing Generalization in Image Forgery
Localization and Informing Model Design
Baoke Dou, Ziye Wang, Hao Wang, Guoqing Cai,
Wende Tan, Chenyang Si, Liucheng Guo, Yueming Lyu
Reliable evaluation of image forgery localization (IFL) requires assessing models under diverse dis-
tribution changes, yet existing benchmarks often cover limited manipulation conditions or entangle
multiple factors in cross-dataset evaluation. Consequently, aggregate performance provides an incom-
plete view of localization generalization. We introduce GIFTBench, a multi-axis benchmark of 115,013
manipulated images with pixel-level annotations spanning manipulation source, semantic target, edit-
ing operation, and composition complexity. GIFTBench supports axis-specific transfer analysis and
evaluation on twelve external datasets. Its diagnostic studies reveal asymmetric cross-source transfer,
recall-dominated failures, and heterogeneous degradation across semantic, operational, and composi-
tional changes. Beyond diagnosis, the scale and diversity of GIFTBench provide a substantially broader
training distribution than conventional IFL datasets. Training representative localizers on GIFTBench
consistently improves their aggregate transfer to external datasets, showing that the benchmark serves
not only as an evaluation tool but also as an effective training resource for cross-domain localization.
Guided by the diagnostic findings, we further develop ForenScope, a detection and localization
framework combining classification-adapted representations with multi-depth, multi-scale spatial
features, learned layer fusion, and selective coarse-scale conditioning. Experiments show improved
cross-dataset localization while retaining image-level detection capability. The GIFTBench dataset
showcase page is available at https://giftbench-preview.doudoudouya337.chatgpt.site.

Introduction
Image manipulation tools have made it increasingly easy to alter image content while preserv-
ing a visually plausible appearance. A complete forensic system should therefore answer two
complementary questions: whether an image is real or manipulated and where the manipulation
occurs. The former corresponds to image-level manipulation detection, while the latter is typi-
cally formulated as image forgery localization (IFL), which predicts a pixel-level manipulation
mask. Although recent methods achieve strong performance on established benchmarks, existing
evaluation does not fully characterize how they generalize under diverse distribution changes
beyond their training data. That distribution shift reduces performance is unsurprising; the more
informative question is which change makes a trained localizer fail.
Existing datasets vary in image content, manipulation source, semantic target, editing operation,
and composition [Dong et al., 2013, Hsu et al., 2006, Wen et al., 2016, Guan et al., 2019, Novozamsky
et al., 2020, Tralic et al., 2013], but these factors are commonly entangled across datasets or only
partially annotated within them. As Table 1 shows, many benchmarks already contain large
collections of classical or AI-based edits and substantial manipulation diversity. The limitation
is therefore not simply dataset scale, but how this diversity is represented and used. Without a
shared factorized annotation space, cross-dataset evaluation can reveal that performance drops
but provides limited evidence about which variation is associated with the failure. At the same
time, a useful benchmark should provide sufficiently diverse and consistently annotated data to
serve as a reusable training resource rather than only an analysis tool. We therefore argue for a
benchmark that jointly represents multiple manipulation factors, supports axis-specific evaluation,
and provides a broad training distribution for studying and improving cross-domain localization.

To support both structured evaluation and diverse model training, we introduce GIFTBench,
a multi-axis benchmark built from ADE20K with 115,013 manipulated images and pixel-level
masks. Each sample is annotated by manipulation source, semantic target, editing operation,
and composition complexity, covering diverse scenes together with both classical and AI-based
manipulations. Rather than constructing separate datasets for individual factors, GIFTBench
organizes these variations within a unified generation and annotation framework, yielding a
coherent training distribution with diversity across multiple levels. The structured annotations
enable axis-specific transfer analysis, while the complete training set can be used directly to train
localization models. Generalization beyond the benchmark is further evaluated on twelve external
datasets.
The axis-specific protocols reveal asymmetric cross-source transfer, recall-dominated failures,
and heterogeneous degradation across semantic, operational, and compositional shifts. Beyond
diagnosis, training representative localizers on the complete GIFTBench set improves aggregate
transfer to external datasets over their reference training distributions, showing that its diversity
benefits both generalization evaluation and transferable model learning.
We develop ForenScope, a joint detection–localization framework built on a classification-adapted
DINOv2 representation [Oquab et al., 2024]. Its localization branch combines multi-depth patch
features, learnable cross-layer fusion, bounded global-to-local modulation, and coarse-to-fine
decoding; the design rationale and empirical tests are presented later.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We introduce GIFTBench, a large-scale, multi-axis benchmark with diverse scenes and ma-
nipulations organized within a unified construction framework, supporting both structured
generalization evaluation and model training.
• We show that GIFTBench enables systematic diagnosis of localization generalization, revealing
asymmetric cross-source transfer, recall-dominated failures, and heterogeneous semantic, oper-
ational, and compositional degradation. Training on the complete GIFTBench distribution also
improves aggregate transfer across external datasets.
• We develop and evaluate ForenScope, a detection and localization model that retains image-
level detection while improving cross-dataset localization.

Related Work
Image manipulation localization benchmarks.
Early IFL benchmarks focused on splicing, copy-
move, and limited post-processing artifacts [Dong et al., 2013, Hsu et al., 2006, Wen et al., 2016,

Guan et al., 2019]. Later datasets broadened manipulation sources and included more realistic
in-the-wild images [Novozamsky et al., 2020, Tralic et al., 2013]. Subsequent benchmarks further
increased scale and manipulation diversity [Mahfoudi et al., 2019, Kadam et al., 2021], while recent
work has increasingly addressed generative and instruction-driven editing [Jia et al., 2023, Mareen
et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2025, Cai et al., 2026].
IFL models.
IFL systems use constrained convolutions, noise or frequency cues, patch reasoning,
transformer encoders, and segmentation decoders [Bayar and Stamm, 2018, Wu et al., 2019, Chen
et al., 2021, Hu et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2022, Ma et al., 2023]. More recent approaches use
foundation-model representations and stronger multimodal or generative priors [Guillaro et al.,
2023, Su et al., 2025, Zhu et al., 2025, Ma et al., 2024].
Foundation models for dense prediction.
Large self-supervised vision encoders provide se-
mantically rich features for dense prediction. General segmentation systems have shown that
transformer representations can support accurate masks when combined with task-specific de-
coders [Kirillov et al., 2023, Ravi et al., 2025]. In image forensics, however, semantically invariant
representations may suppress weak manipulation-specific evidence.

GIFTBench: Multi-Axis Benchmark for Generalization Diagnosis
3.1
Benchmark Design
GIFTBench studies image forgery localization under benchmark-defined distribution shifts by
annotating manipulation variation along four explicit dimensions and evaluating generalization
separately with respect to each. Its design follows three principles: major sources of shift should
be explicitly labeled rather than hidden within a random split; manipulation masks should have
deterministic provenance; and the benchmark should support both axis-specific analysis and
transfer to established external datasets.
Accordingly, each manipulated image is characterized along four complementary axes:
• Manipulation source: splicing, copy–move, removal, and AI editing;
• Semantic target: object-, component-, and background-level manipulation;
• Editing operation: adding, removing, and replacing content;
• Composition complexity: single manipulation, fusion of multiple edits, and multi-source
composition.
Manipulation source describes the editing pipeline or generation family used to produce a sample,
whereas editing operation describes the intended transformation applied to the image. The two
annotations are distinct: Removal denotes a source-family pipeline, while Remove denotes an editing
objective that may also be realized by other source families. Each sample retains both labels and
can therefore be evaluated from either diagnostic perspective. The first three axes characterize the
origin, spatial semantics, and intent of an edit, while the composition axis captures the interaction
of multiple manipulation processes or regions within the same image. Together, these factors
define the benchmark’s multi-axis evaluation space.
Construction summary.
GIFTBench uses ADE20K images and semantic annotations [Zhou et al.,
2019] to generate manipulations through vision–language-guided planning and multiple editing
pipelines, while retaining explicit masks and provenance verified by automatic and manual quality
checks. Before generation, all primary and donor sources are assigned to disjoint train or test pools,
which are inherited by every generated sample and its source material. The benchmark contains

3.2
Evaluation Protocols
GIFTBench defines four axis-specific diagnostic protocols. Each organizes training and evaluation
along one benchmark axis under common evaluation rules, without requiring the remaining
annotations to be statistically matched. Results should therefore be interpreted as axis-associated
transfer patterns rather than strict one-variable-at-a-time interventions.
• Protocol I: Manipulation-source transfer. Models are trained on one source and evaluated on
four manipulation sources, yielding a 4 × 4 transfer matrix.
• Protocol II: Semantic Target transfer. Models are trained and evaluated across three semantic
target levels, yielding a 3 × 3 transfer matrix.
• Protocol III: Editing-operation transfer. Models are trained and tested across three operations,
yielding a 3 × 3 transfer matrix.
• Protocol IV: Composition generalization. Models are trained on single-operation examples

and evaluated on single, fusion, and multi-source manipulations to assess generalization to
composed settings.
In addition to the axis-specific protocols, we evaluate two forms of external transfer: released check-
points are applied directly to GIFTBench without adaptation, while GIFTBench-trained models are
evaluated on external datasets without target-domain fine-tuning. The former diagnoses the OOD
transfer of existing systems, whereas the latter assesses generalization beyond the GIFTBench con-
struction domain. Pixel-level F1 and IoU are averaged over manipulated images, and cross-dataset
results are macro-averaged over datasets. Real images are excluded from localization metrics;
image-level detection is evaluated separately on datasets containing both real and manipulated
images.
3.3
Benchmark Diagnosis
Existing models exhibit a substantial transfer gap.
We analyze axis-specific failures by retraining
representative CNN-, Transformer-, and foundation-model-based localizers with identical source-
image splits, thresholds, and metrics. For released-checkpoint transfer, we use Protocol-MVSS and
Protocol-CAT checkpoints from IMDL-BenCo [Ma et al., 2024] and the MMTD-Set checkpoint from
FakeShield [Xu et al., 2025a]. Transfer is markedly stronger on Splicing and Copy–Move than on
Removal and AI–Edit. The same architecture behaves differently across training protocols, while
model rankings vary among source and composition groups. Thus, OOD performance depends
on both the training distribution and target condition and cannot be adequately characterized by a
single aggregate score. Complete results are provided in Appendix Table 9.
Across the source, semantic-target, and operation protocols, transfer varies with the train–test
pairing; Appendix Fig. 6 provides a compact visualization of these cross-domain asymmetries.
Figure 3 analyzes the failure mechanisms beyond aggregate scores. In panel (a), off-diagonal
source transfers are dominated by false negatives while false positives remain low, and recall drops
sharply relative to matched evaluation. Unfamiliar source traces are therefore chiefly missed rather
than producing indiscriminate foreground predictions. Panel (b) shows that semantic-target
groups also differ in their forensic statistics: background edits tend to occupy larger regions and
exhibit weaker boundary gradients and lower high-frequency energy than object- and component-
level edits. The semantic transfer gap thus coincides with changes in spatial extent, texture, and
boundary strength rather than with category labels alone.
Panel (c) further reveals operation-dependent spatial failures. Add-trained models show relatively
balanced boundary and core recall degradation, Remove-trained models lose more core recall,
and Replace-trained models lose more boundary recall, indicating different reliance on interior
and boundary evidence. Controlled single-to-composition evaluation also degrades across models
(Appendix Table 13); panel (d) illustrates how co-occurring manipulations can redirect SparseViT
attention from the original target toward competing visual regions, producing incomplete or
expanded predictions. Together, these analyses show that generalization fails through distinct
spatial mechanisms: missed unfamiliar cues, altered region statistics, boundary/interior sensitivity,
and attention competition under composition.

ForenScope: Benchmark-Informed Detection and Localization
4.1
Overview
The GIFTBench diagnosis further indicates that manipulation evidence can be source sensitive,
spatially heterogeneous under composition, and easily missed when local cues become ambiguous.
These observations do not uniquely determine an architecture; instead, they suggest three design
requirements: avoiding dependence on a single representation, modeling evidence across spatial
extents, and allowing uncertain local predictions to exploit complementary image-level context.
ForenScope instantiates these requirements as a joint detection–localization model with multi-

Image-level detection and pixel-level localization provide complementary views of manipulation
evidence: patch features indicate where an edit may occur, whereas the CLS representation
summarizes whether the image contains manipulation cues globally. When local evidence is
weak, this global representation can provide a useful prior; however, using it as a hard mask gate

4.4
Training and Inference
For each training protocol, the DINOv2 encoder is first adapted for image-level manipulation
classification on the corresponding training data using LoRA. The adapted encoder and inherited
classification head are then frozen, and the localization-specific modules are trained on the same
data with pixel-level supervision. This preserves the image-level representation while allowing
the localization branch to learn how to reuse it.
Auxiliary heads supervise H19, H37, and H74 so that every spatial pathway remains directly
predictive instead of relying only on the final fusion output. Let ℓs denote the weighted sum of
binary cross-entropy and soft Dice loss at output scale (s), and let λs denote the corresponding
auxiliary-loss coefficient. The localization objective is

pendix D. No classification loss is optimized during localization training; the frozen classification
branch remains available for image-level detection, while its class representation provides global
context to the localization decoder.
Optional prototype-guided refinement.
The direct mask can still contain locally uncertain
predictions even when its confident regions are reliable. We therefore study an inference-only
prototype refinement applied to the direct ForenScope prediction. Using the H37 features and
resized localization scores, manipulated and real prototypes are estimated from confident regions,
and their cosine-similarity margin is used to adjust the final logits. The refinement is applied
with the same fixed rule across datasets, requires no target-domain labels or parameter updates,
and is reported as an inference-only extension of the direct prediction. Full formulation and
hyperparameters are provided in Appendix D.7.

Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setup
We evaluate ForenScope for image-level manipulation detection and pixel-level localization under
two independently trained settings. The GIFTBench setting performs both training stages on
the GIFTBench training split, whereas Protocol-MVSS repeats them using only CASIAv2; the
settings share neither training data nor adapted checkpoints. All OOD datasets are reserved for
evaluation, and checkpoints are selected using validation data from the corresponding training
domain. Unless otherwise stated, ablations follow the GIFTBench setting.
At inference, detection and localization are predicted separately; the detection decision does not
gate the mask, although CLS features condition the localization branch through FiLM. Unless
otherwise specified, ForenScope denotes the direct output of the selected EMA checkpoint at
a fixed threshold of 0.5. Localization F1 and IoU are computed on manipulated images, while
image-level ROC AUC is reported on datasets containing both real and manipulated samples.
ForenScope + Proto. denotes the fixed inference-only refinement. Full implementation details are
provided in Appendix D.
5.2
Overall Performance
Table 3 reports pixel-level F1 on three held-out GIFTBench composition subsets and nine OOD
datasets; complete in-domain and twelve-dataset F1/IoU results are provided in Appendix E. On
the source-disjoint GIFTBench split, ForenScope remains strong across source, semantic-target,
operation, and composition views. Across the nine OOD datasets, the direct ForenScope output
achieves the highest average F1 among the compared direct-output models and outperforms
SparseViT on most datasets, despite dataset-specific variation.
The lower panel reports cross-dataset results under Protocol-MVSS training. ForenScope achieves
the highest average F1 among the compared methods, although its relative advantage varies across
individual OOD datasets. The optional prototype refinement further improves the direct prediction
and produces the strongest overall performance in this setting.
Image-level detection.
Although our architectural contributions focus on localization, Foren-
Scope retains the image-level classifier inherited from the classification-adapted encoder. On the
eight external datasets containing both real and manipulated images, the GIFTBench-adapted
detector achieves an average ROC AUC of 0.935 and F1 of 0.871. The full per-dataset comparison,
including the controlled CASIAv2-trained setting, is provided in Appendix E.

Conclusion
We presented GIFTBench, a multi-axis benchmark for diagnosing image forgery localization
generalization, together with ForenScope, a detection–localization model using learned depth–
scale fusion and bounded global conditioning. GIFTBench exposes distinct transfer behavior
across manipulation source, semantic target, operation, and composition while complementing
controlled diagnosis with twelve-dataset external evaluation. ForenScope retains image-level
detection and improves cross-dataset localization, demonstrating how structured benchmark
diagnosis can provide testable guidance for more transferable forensic models.

Benchmark Construction and Data Specification
This appendix section specifies what GIFTBench contains, how each manipulated sample is con-
structed, and how split membership, masks, and provenance are recorded. It supplements the
benchmark overview in the main paper with the details needed to reconstruct protocol member-
ships without repeating the main motivation.
A.1
Scope, taxonomy, and task definition
GIFTBench is derived from ADE20K training images and their semantic annotations [Zhou et al.,
2019]. Its purpose is to keep the real-image universe and localization target controlled while
exposing several sources of distribution shift that are usually entangled in existing IFL datasets.
Each manipulated image has a split-assigned primary source image and, where applicable, split-
assigned donor or component sources; it is also assigned metadata for manipulation source,
semantic target, editing operation, composition level, split, construction configuration, and quality-
control status. Multi-Source samples additionally retain component-level masks and provenance.
The four axes are defined as follows:

These labels are intentionally not interchangeable. Removal in the source axis denotes a family
of removal pipelines, whereas Remove in the operation axis denotes an editing objective that can
also be instantiated by other source families. Similarly, Fusion and Multi-Source are composition
levels derived from the same source-image universe, not independent image collections. This
representation allows one sample to be grouped by different axes in different diagnostic protocols
while keeping its image, mask, and provenance fixed.
The released image-level label indicates whether an image is real or manipulated, while the pixel-
level target is the binary mask of pixels changed by the recorded construction operation. Source-
family, semantic-target, operation, and composition labels are metadata used to form controlled
evaluation groups; they are not additional prediction targets in the localization experiments. The
primary source identifier links an image and its mask, while donor and component provenance is
retained when applicable; an image can therefore be assigned to different protocol views without
changing its supervision.
A.2
Construction pipeline
The construction process begins with a real ADE20K image and its semantic annotations. Human
experts define the taxonomy, admissible combinations, and construction constraints. A vision–
language model then analyzes the scene and produces a structured manipulation plan, including
the target region, source material from the assigned split, intended operation, and spatial attributes.
A family-specific editing engine performs the pixel-level change, after which automatic checks,
VLM-based verification, and manual review validate the image, mask, and metadata. Figure 2
summarizes this construction pipeline.
The semantic taxonomy and admissible group assignments are fixed before generation. Candidate

selection and editing use the recorded random state for the corresponding construction job, whereas
mask derivation, normalization, quality checks, and split assignment are deterministic once
the construction record is fixed. Geometric transforms are applied jointly to image and mask;
photometric transforms are applied only to the image. Masks are resized with nearest-neighbor
interpolation and re-binarized after every geometry-changing operation. These rules prevent
augmentation from changing the localization target while preserving the intended appearance
variation.
Semantic planning and material selection.
VLMs are used as semantic controllers rather than
as the sole pixel generators. Qwen-VL and InternVL-family models [Bai et al., 2025b,a, Wang et al.,
2025a] identify eligible regions and contextual relations, propose or verify target regions and source
materials, and assess placement, scale, occlusion, illumination, and scene compatibility where
applicable. Candidate source contents are filtered for geometric completeness and contextual
compatibility within the assigned split before execution. This separation lets the benchmark vary
the editing engine without making the entire release an artifact of one VLM.
Pixel-level execution and mask generation.
Classical compositing, traditional or learned inpaint-
ing, and diffusion-based editors perform the actual image modification. The executor is selected
according to the source family and the semantic characteristics of the target. A binary mask is
generated from the recorded construction operation and identifies pixels changed relative to the
original source. For composed samples, masks for the individual components are retained in
addition to the final union or refined mask.
A.3
Manipulation source instantiation
Splicing.
Splicing includes object insertion or replacement and background replacement. Back-
ground edits include partial-region, whole-region, and cross-class substitutions. For object edits,
the structured plan specifies the candidate object, placement, support surface, scale, lighting
relation, and occlusion constraints; compatible donor material is selected from the ADE20K pool
assigned to the same train/test partition as the primary source before classical compositing executes
the edit. These cases vary both local blending traces and scene-level consistency requirements.
Copy–Move.
Copy–Move includes background-to-background texture copying, background-
to-object overwriting, whole-object relocation, superpixel-level cloning, texture extension, and
the attribute subset. Candidate regions are selected according to semantic role and geometric
completeness, followed by controlled transformations, color alignment, and blending. The attribute
subset is categorized as Copy–Move in the release taxonomy and is included in the corresponding
source-level counts.
Removal.
Removal covers background-texture repair, structural-object removal, and semantic-
foreground removal. The construction plan distinguishes locally repairable textures from extended
structures and complete foreground instances. Traditional inpainting [Telea, 2004] or learned
inpainting [Suvorov et al., 2022, Li et al., 2022, Sargsyan et al., 2023, Jain et al., 2023, Li et al., 2025]
is selected according to the target’s spatial and semantic characteristics, so the source family is not
restricted to one inpainting mechanism.
AI-Edit.
AI-Edit samples use mask-conditioned diffusion editing driven by visually grounded,
structured instructions. The plans cover subject-level, background/stuff-level, component-level,
and spatial-interaction edits; each can implement Add, Remove, or Replace. Multiple diffusion
and instruction-guided inpainting backends [Rombach et al., 2022, Podell et al., 2024, Manukyan

et al., 2023, Zhuang et al., 2024, Batifol et al., 2025] are used to provide variation in generative
mechanisms, while semantic target and operation remain separately recorded.
A.4
Composition construction
Composition is not treated as a fifth manipulation source. It tests whether evidence learned from
isolated edits survives two controlled changes in the same source-image setting. Fusion begins
with a single-operation manipulation and applies latent-diffusion inpainting [Rombach et al., 2022,
Podell et al., 2024] only to a narrow morphological band around the existing manipulation bound-
ary; pixels outside the band remain unchanged. The final mask includes the refined boundary
band and the retained preceding manipulation, providing a matched single-operation reference.
Multi-Source composition combines two to four distinct single-manipulation components derived
from the same real image and its assigned source pool. The visible components are placed back onto
the original background with controlled spatial layouts, and their masks are merged into a final
union mask. Component-level masks and provenance support analyses beyond the union-mask
localization target. The composition training partitions are released for future composition-aware
methods, whereas the Protocol IV baselines in this paper train only on GIFTBench-Core and
therefore measure zero-composition generalization.
A.5
Quality assurance, masks, and provenance
Before large-scale generation, each manipulation family and its mask-extraction procedure is
checked on pilot examples for visual plausibility and mask correctness. During generation, sam-
ples whose manipulated region is below 0.5% of the image area are discarded. Candidate sam-
ples are further filtered by geometry, manipulated-area, semantic consistency, visual quality,
saliency change, and near-duplicate content. AI-Edit candidates additionally undergo BRISQUE
no-reference quality assessment, used for relative ranking within each batch rather than with a
fixed threshold, and CLIP-based semantic similarity between the edited region and the target
instruction.
All remaining samples receive VLM-based quality assessment. Classical manipulations use three-
level scoring (1: superior, 2: acceptable, 3: discard), while AI-Edit samples receive type-specific
binary verification of physical plausibility and image–instruction consistency. Samples with low
saliency relative to the source, low visual quality, or duplicated scene content are removed. Finally,
retained samples are manually inspected for incorrect edits, implausible results, and inaccurate
masks. These checks prioritize reliable localization targets rather than only maximizing the number
of generated images.
The released mask is a single-channel binary image with values 0 and 255. Construction-time
grayscale masks are normalized at release time using threshold 128: values ≥128 are encoded
as 255 and all other values as 0. Positive pixels denote changes relative to the original source
under the recorded construction procedure. The construction pipeline initially produced 115,025
candidates; twelve Copy–Move training candidates with no positive pixels after normalization
were excluded as invalid localization targets, yielding the final release of 115,013 samples. The
excluded records are documented in the release audit and are not treated as a separate benchmark
category.
Each release record stores the manipulated-image identifier, original source identifier, mask, four-
axis labels, split, construction engine and configuration identifier, and quality-control decision.
For Multi-Source samples, component masks and component-level provenance are also retained.
This record structure allows protocol memberships and dataset statistics to be regenerated from a
common manifest.

B.2
Metric and aggregation definitions
For each manipulated image, the predicted probability map is thresholded at 0.5 and compared
with the binary target mask. Pixel-level F1 and IoU are computed independently for each manip-
ulated image and then averaged within each dataset or benchmark group. Real images are not
assigned a manipulated-mask F1/IoU when no positive mask exists; their predicted-positive area
is retained only for image-level false-positive inspection. Cross-dataset summaries macro-average
the dataset-level scores, so All-12 is the equal-weight mean over the twelve external datasets and
the Avg columns in the main tables are equal-weight means over the displayed datasets.
Image-level detection uses the frozen CLS head score and ROC AUC. AUC is defined only when
both real and manipulated images are present in the evaluated split; the four pure-manipulated
external splits therefore receive “–” rather than a fabricated AUC. The image-level score and
localization mask are retained as separate outputs at inference; no post-hoc detector gate is applied
to the mask. Unless a table states otherwise, localization uses the EMA checkpoint, the fixed
threshold above, and the direct ForenScope output. Optional ForenScope + Proto. results apply
the fixed prototype refinement described in Appendix D.7.

D
ForenScope Architecture, Training, and Inference Details
D.1
Classification adaptation with LoRA
Before localization training, DINOv2 is adapted together with a lightweight image-level classifica-
tion head. The adaptation stage starts from the official DINOv2 ViT-L/14-reg pretrained weights
and inserts LoRA adapters into the attention projections of blocks 8–23. The original encoder
weights remain frozen; only the LoRA parameters and the CLS head are optimized with a binary
classification loss. The head consumes the CLS token and predicts the real/manipulated image
label. The LoRA adapters use a low-rank residual parameterization with a small update scale and
dropout, while the classification head uses a larger learning rate than the adapters. Classification
checkpoints are selected using the available classification validation protocol.
We use two data configurations for this stage. The standard ForenScope configuration performs
the adaptation on the ADE-domain classification data and provides the ADE-adapted encoder
and CLS head used by the main localization model. The controlled Protocol-MVSS configuration
repeats the same adaptation recipe from the official pretrained encoder on CASIAv2, using its real
and manipulated images, and then initializes the subsequent localization stage from the resulting
CASIAv2-adapted checkpoint. CASIAv1 is not used to optimize the CASIAv2 classification adapta-
tion. These configurations change the source of the adapted representation, while the downstream
localization architecture and the freezing rule remain the same.
After adaptation, the encoder, its LoRA parameters, and the CLS head are frozen during localization
training. The localization decoder consumes patch features from the adapted encoder, while
the frozen CLS representation is retained for image-level detection and for CLS-conditioned
modulation of the localization branch. Thus, the classification adaptation is part of the initialization
pipeline rather than an additional loss optimized during localization.

D.3
Encoder and token extraction
The encoder is DINOv2 ViT-L/14 with four register tokens. At input resolution 1036 × 1036, the
token sequence is ordered as
CLS, register1, . . . , register4, patch1, . . . , patchN.
The classifier consumes the CLS token. The localization branch removes the first 1 + 4 tokens
and reshapes only the remaining patch tokens into spatial maps. Feature taps are the zero-based
DINOv2 block indices F8, F11, F14, F17; register tokens are never treated as spatial patches.
D.4
Multi-scale projection and learnable fusion
Each of the four tapped feature maps is projected from 1024 to 128 channels by an independent
1 × 1 projection followed by GroupNorm and GELU. The four projected features are mapped to
the three localization resolutions H19, H37, and H74, producing twelve layer–scale lateral paths.
Scale-specific downsampling or upsampling preserves the spatial correspondence of each path
before fusion.
For each scale, the LayerMixer maintains four learnable logits and normalizes them with a softmax.
The resulting output is a four-way convex mixture Fs = P

optimized jointly with the localization branch. The fixed-equal intervention in Appendix E.4
replaces these learned coefficients by 0.25 for each depth at inference without retraining.

D.6
Objective, sampling, augmentation, optimization, and selection
The localization objective is binary cross-entropy plus soft Dice at the final output and auxiliary
scales:
L = ℓfinal + 0.1ℓ19 + 0.2ℓ37 + 0.3ℓ74.
Manipulated images have weight 1.0 and real images have weight 0.3. The main training protocol
samples 17,000 examples from each of Splicing, Copy–Move, Removal, and AI-Edit, includes 532
attribute examples, and samples 20,000 real images, for a planned 88,532 samples per epoch (88,512
effective samples under the three-process DDP run). Validation contains 493 manipulated and 493
real images.
The formal configuration uses three-process DDP with batch size 8 per process (global batch size
24), two data-loader workers per process, five epochs, learning rate 3 × 10−4, and seed 42. Only
the 3,210,256 localization-branch parameters are optimized. We use AdamW with weight decay

All twelve external datasets are excluded from optimization, validation, and checkpoint selection;

D.7

direct ForenScope output. It does not update the encoder, decoder, optimizer state, or checkpoint.

1. obtain the direct localization logits and the normalized H37 patch features;
2. use the direct H37 prediction to form manipulated and real support features with the fixed
70/30 trimmed support rule;
