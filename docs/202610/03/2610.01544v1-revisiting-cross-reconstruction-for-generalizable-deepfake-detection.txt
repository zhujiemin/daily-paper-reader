Abstract 

Existing image forgery detectors often suffer from general-ization to unseen manipulation methods due to the limited ability to capture transferable forensic cues. Recent cross-reconstruction based methods attempt to improve generaliza-tion through semantic-artifact disentanglement, but typically align heterogeneous artifacts across generators and exclude ar-tifact representations during reconstruction, which may over-look the inherent diversity and visual cues of manipulation ar-tifacts. In this work, we revisit cross-reconstruction and intro-duce an artifact-oriented disentanglement framework for ro-bust image forgery detection. We argue that artifact diversity ,i.e., the intrinsic variations of manipulation artifacts intro-duced by different generation processes, contains complemen-tary forensic cues rather than undesirable domain variations. Instead of enforcing explicit artifact alignment, our frame-work preserves diverse artifact characteristics through seman-tically aligned cross-generator reconstruction. Furthermore, we incorporate artifact representations into the reconstruc-tion process and introduce a masked frequency-aware recon-struction strategy to emphasize manipulation-related residuals while reducing semantic interference. This design enables the model to learn transferable forensic representations from di-verse artifacts. Extensive experiments on multiple benchmark datasets demonstrate improvements under both cross-dataset and cross-generator evaluation settings. Further analysis and ablation studies validate the effectiveness of artifact diversity preservation and artifact-aware cross-reconstruction. 

# Introduction 

Recent image forgery detectors have increasingly explored disentangled representation learning to separate semantic in-formation from manipulation artifacts for better generaliza-tion. Among them, cross-reconstruction based methods (Yan et al. 2023a; Lin et al. 2024) construct paired real-fake sam-ples and decompose their representations into semantic and artifact components. By swapping artifact representations while preserving semantic representations, they reconstruct the original RGB images to encourage semantic-artifact dis-entanglement. They further divide artifact representations into generator-related and generator-invariant components, where the former is supervised by generator classification and the latter is utilized for forgery detection. However, we argue that such paradigm overlooks an impor-tant property of forgery artifacts, namely artifact diversity as

Artifact diversity refers to the intrinsic vari-ations of manipulation artifacts introduced by different gen-eration models, training strategies, and synthesis pipelines. Although these artifacts exhibit heterogeneous distributions across generators, they often contain complementary foren-sic cues that reveal different aspects of the manipulation process. Therefore, artifact diversity should not be regarded as undesirable domain variation that needs to be eliminated, but rather as valuable forensic knowledge that should be pre-served and exploited. As illustrated in Fig. 1, existing cross-reconstruction meth-ods tend to align artifacts from different generators into a shared representation space. While this strategy improves arXiv:2610.01544v1 [cs.CV] 1 Oct 2026 invariance to seen generators, it may suppress generator-specific but transferable forensic cues and lead to overfit-ting. Consequently, the learned representation can be biased toward the artifact statistics of training generators and fail to capture the diverse forensic patterns required for unseen manipulations. Besides artifact alignment, another limita-tion lies in the role of artifacts during reconstruction. Exist-ing methods typically exclude artifact representations from reconstruction process, assuming that artifacts are merely reconstruction-irrelevant noise. However, forgery artifacts are not merely reconstruction-irrelevant noise. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a)-(b), they contain meaningful visual information, such as color mismatch and detail distortion, providing com-plementary forensic cues for identifying manipulated images. Therefore, whether artifacts should participate in reconstruc-tion remains an open question. To address these limitations, we propose an artifact-oriented disentanglement framework for robust image forgery detection. Instead of enforcing explicit artifact align-ment, our method preserves artifact diversity and learns transferable forensic representations from heterogeneous ar-tifacts. We redesign cross-reconstruction by incorporating ar-tifact representations as reconstruction guidance. To enhance artifact supervision, we introduce a masked frequency-aware reconstruction strategy that suppresses semantic-dominant components while emphasizing manipulation-related resid-uals. Extensive experiments on multiple benchmarks demon-strate consistent improvements under cross-dataset settings. Ablation studies further validate the effectiveness of artifact diversity preservation and artifact-aware reconstruction. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We revisit cross-reconstruction for deepfake detection and identify two limitations: aligning heterogeneous artifacts suppresses artifact diversity, while the exclusion of arti-fact representations from reconstruction overlooks some forensic cues. 

• We propose an artifact-aware disentanglement frame-work using semantically aligned cross-generator pairs. Cross-reconstruction incorporates artifact representa-tions into the reconstruction process, enabling the ex-ploitation of diverse manipulation cues. Meanwhile, masked high-frequency residual reconstruction empha-sizes manipulation-related details and promotes transfer-able forensic representation learning. 

• Extensive cross-dataset experiments demonstrate com-petitive state-of-the-art performance. Ablation studies further validate the benefits of artifact diversity preser-vation and artifact-aware cross-reconstruction. 

# Related Works 

Generalizable Deepfake Detection 

Existing deepfake detectors (Li et al. 2023; Ba et al. 2024; Concas et al. 2024) have achieved remarkable performance under in-domain evaluation, yet their generalization abil-ity remains limited when encountering unseen manipula-tion methods or datasets. To alleviate this issue, recent stud-ies(Zhu et al. 2026; Chou et al. 2026) have explored various strategies for learning more generalizable representations. Some works (Stehouwer et al. 2019; Li et al. 2020a; Nguyen et al. 2024) exploit forgery artifacts in the spatial and fre-quency domains, such as blending inconsistencies, abnor-mal noise patterns, and spectral artifacts. Another direc-tion introduces large-scale pre-trained vision models, such as Xception(Chollet 2017) and CLIP(Radford et al. 2021) into deepfake detection to leverage their powerful visual rep-resentations and superior transferability. Effort (Yan et al. 2024b) explicitly enforces orthogonality between semantic and forgery subspaces based on CLIP representations. CFB (Li et al. 2025) performs multi-scale feature-level disentan-glement of semantic and artifact representations using an Xception backbone. Beyond semantic-artifact disentangle-ment, recent studies(Lin et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2026) further decompose forensic representations into generator-specific and generator-shared components to improve cross-generator generalization. Such methods assume that the shared arti-fact space can be effectively learned from a limited number of seen generators. However, given the limited diversity of generators available during training and the rapid evolution of deepfake generation techniques, this assumption may be overly restrictive and potentially lead to over-specialized rep-resentations. We therefore argue that preserving the diversity of forgery artifacts, rather than enforcing a predefined shared artifact space, may provide a more effective path toward ro-bust generalization. 

Reconstruction Based Representation Learning in Deepfake Detection 

Reconstruction based representation learning(Kim and Mnih 2018; Lee et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2026) has been widely adopted to disentangle latent factors and learn discrimina-tive feature representations. By reconstructing samples from latent representations, these methods encourage different representation subspaces to capture complementary infor-mation while suppressing nuisance factors. Inspired by its success in representation disentanglement, recent deepfake detection methods have incorporated reconstruction to sep-arate semantic content from forgery-related representations. UCF (Yan et al. 2023a) introduces cross-reconstruction to uncover common forgery features shared across different manipulation methods, encouraging the detector to focus on manipulation-relevant information. Similarly, PFG(Lin et al. 2024) employs cross-reconstruction to disentangle de-mographic attributes from forgery representations, thereby learning domain-agnostic forensic features for improved cross-domain generalization. CFB(Li et al. 2025) extends reconstruction from the image level to the feature level, enabling multi-scale disentanglement of semantic and arti-fact representations. Despite their effectiveness, the recon-struction objective is designed in a task-agnostic manner rather than being tailored for forensic representation learn-ing. Given that forgery artifacts are predominantly mani-fested in high-frequency components, directly reconstruct-ing RGB images may provide suboptimal supervision for learning artifact-aware representations. Method 

Overview 

Our framework aims to learn transferable forensic represen-tations by preserving the diversity of manipulation artifacts rather than enforcing explicit artifact alignment. Given se-mantically aligned image pairs generated by different ma-nipulation methods, the framework extracts disentangled se-mantic and artifact representations through a query-based encoder and a decouple head. To exploit the complementary information in diverse artifacts, we introduce a diversity-preserving cross-reconstruction module, where artifact rep-resentations are exchanged and incorporated into recon-struction. Meanwhile, masked high-frequency residuals are adopted as reconstruction targets to emphasize manipulation-related variations and facilitate the learning of transferable forensic representations. For forgery detection, we introduce a CLIP-based clas-sification module guided by artifact-aware attention biases. The entire framework is jointly optimized with classification loss, artifact contrastive loss, and cross-reconstruction loss, enabling effective exploitation of diverse manipulation cues for generalized deepfake detection. 

Training Pair Formation 

We adopt the FaceForensics++ (Rossler et al. 2019) dataset as the training set, since it provides pristine videos together with multiple face manipulation methods generated from the same source videos. Different from conventional cross-reconstruction methods, which typically construct training pairs from real and fake images, our training pairs are com-posed of semantically similar samples generated by different manipulation methods, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Specifically, FF++ dataset consists of pristine videos together with two categories of manipulated videos: face reenactment and face swapping. During pair construction, pristine videos and face reenactment videos are grouped together, while face swap-ping videos are treated as a separate group. For each training pair, two videos are sampled from one group. The paired sam-ples therefore exhibit highly consistent facial pose, expres-sion, and background while originating from different gener-ators. Afterward, frames with identical indices are extracted from each paired video to form image pairs for training. Stan-dard face preprocessing, including face detection, alignment, and cropping, follows the protocol in DeepfakeBench (Yan et al. 2023b).

Decoder. To further enforce the disentanglement between semantic and artifact representations, we introduce a recon-struction decoder. Given the four features produced by de-couple head: {f si , f ai , f sj , f aj }, the decoder performs cross-reconstruction by exchanging artifact representations while preserving semantic representations. Specifically, Adaptive Instance Normalization (AdaIN) (Huang and Belongie 2017) is employed to inject artifact representations into semantic representations: 

AdaIN( f s, f a) = σ(f a) f s − μ(f s)

σ(f s) + μ(f a), (3) where μ(·) and σ(·) denote the channel-wise mean and stan-dard deviation, respectively. The exchanged representations are formulated as: 

Zj←i = AdaIN( f sj , f ai ), Zi←j = AdaIN( f si , f aj ).

(4) The reconstructed images are obtained through a lightweight decoder as: 

ˆxj←i = D(Zj←i), ˆxi←j = D(Zi←j ), (5) where D denotes convolution layers that transform the di-mension of the output as ˆx ∈ Rh×w×3 .

Reconstruction Target. Directly reconstructing RGB im-ages may introduce excessive low-frequency semantic infor-mation into the reconstruction objective, making it difficult to explicitly optimize the manipulation-related artifacts. To alleviate this issue, we redefine the reconstruction target by removing the low-frequency components from the original image. A Gaussian filter is adopted as a low-frequency filter, and the reconstruction target is formulated as: 

xgt = x − G( x), (6) where G( ·) denotes the Gaussian blur operation. By sup-pressing low-frequency information, the reconstructed target mainly preserves high-frequency variations, where manipu-lation artifacts are more prominent. Given the reconstructed target xgt and the prediction ˆx,the reconstruction loss is defined as: 

Lrec = ˆx − xgt . (7) Although the high-frequency reconstruction target empha-sizes manipulation-related artifacts, not all high-frequency components are associated with forgery patterns. The back-ground may contain irrelevant details, such as natural textures and scene details. Therefore, we introduce a spatial mask to focus the reconstruction supervision on manipulation-related regions. Since cross-reconstruction exchanges artifact repre-sentations between paired samples, the reconstructed target should contain artifact responses from both samples. We adopt the union of manipulation regions from the paired samples to cover all potential artifact areas. For a paired sample (xi, x j ), the reconstruction mask is defined as: 

M = Mi ∪ M j , (8) where Mi and Mj denote the binary pixel-level masks of the manipulated regions in the paired samples. The final cross-reconstruction loss is calculated within the manipulated regions: 

Lcross = M ⊙ ( (ˆ xj←i − xgt i ) + (ˆ xi←j − xgt j ) ), (9) where ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication.

# Experiments 

Settings 

Dataset. Following previous works, we train our model on the c23 version of FaceForensics++ (FF++) dataset(Rossler et al. 2019). For evaluation, we conduct cross-dataset ex-periments on widely-used benchmarks, including Celeb-DF-v1/v2 (Li et al. 2020b), DFDCP (Dolhansky et al. 2019), DFDC (Dolhansky et al. 2020), DFD (Dufour and Gully 2019), and four full-face synthesis datasets in DF40 (Yan et al. 2024c), including VQGAN (Esser, Rombach, and Om-mer 2021), StyleGAN-XL (StyleGAN) (Sauer, Schwarz, and Geiger 2022), SiT-XL/2 (SiT) (Atito, Awais, and Kittler 2021), and DiT (Peebles and Xie 2023). 

Implementation Details. We use ViT-Tiny/16 (Dosovit-skiy et al. 2020) for visual feature extraction and a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 (Radford et al. 2021) for classification. The entire model is trained on the FF++ c23 training set with a batch size of 16, using the Adam optimizer (Kingma and Ba 2014) with a learning rate of 0.0002 and a weight decay of 0.0005. The overall objective is optimized with a weighted combination of classification loss, artifact contrastive loss, and cross-reconstruction loss, where the loss weights λ1, λ2,and λ3 are set to 10, 20, and 50, respectively. Detection per-formance is evaluated by AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve), AP (Average Precision), and EER (Equal Error Rate).The random seed is fixed to 1020 for reproducible experiments. All experiments are conducted on one NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

Impact of Cross-Reconstruction Settings. We investigate the impact of different cross-reconstruction designs, includ-ing the choice of reconstruction pairs and targets. As shown in Table 5, we establish a baseline using real-fake pairs with RGB image reconstruction. By replacing real-fake pairs with semantically identical fake pairs generated by different ma-nipulation methods, the detection performance is improved on most datasets. The AUC on CDF-v1 increases from 87.1% to 90.5%, demonstrating that fake-fake pairs provide a more effective way to capture diverse forensic artifacts while re-ducing semantic interference. Furthermore, replacing RGB reconstruction with residual reconstruction further enhances the generalization capabil-ity by focusing on high-frequency forensic cues rather than image content. Combining both designs, i.e., fake-fake pairs with residual reconstruction, achieves the best overall per-formance, obtaining the highest AUC on four out of five benchmark datasets and improving the AUC on CDF-v1 from 87.1% to 93.0%. These results verify that the proposed cross-reconstruction strategy benefits from both artifact-aware pair construction and residual-level supervision, enabling the model to learn more generalizable forensic representations beyond dataset-specific appearance information.

# Conclusion 

In this work, we revisit cross-reconstruction for image forgery detection and highlight the importance of artifact diversity in improving generalization. Unlike previous approaches that attempt to align heterogeneous artifacts or exclude them from reconstruction, we demonstrate that diverse manip-ulation artifacts contain complementary forensic cues that should be preserved and exploited. Based on this insight, we propose an artifact-aware disentanglement framework that leverages semantically aligned cross-generator pairs. By in-corporating artifact representations into cross-reconstruction and introducing masked high-frequency residual targets, our framework learns transferable forensic representations while maintaining diverse artifact characteristics. Extensive experi-ments on multiple benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach under both cross-dataset and cross-generator evaluation settings. References
