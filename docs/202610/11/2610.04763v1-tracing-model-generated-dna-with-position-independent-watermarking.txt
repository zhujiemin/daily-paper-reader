# Tracing model-generated DNA with position-independent watermarking 

## Kimon Antonios Provatas 1,* Christos Galanopoulos 1,* 

## Ilias Georgakopoulos-Soares 1,†  

> 1The University of Texas at Austin, Austin, Texas, USA
> *These authors contributed equally. †Correspondence: ilias@austin.utexas.edu

Abstract. Genomic language models can write synthetic DNA that carries no record of its origin. Ageneration-time watermark could provide a provenance signal, if a verifier can detect it using only the DNA sequence, a key, and published detector settings, without knowing where the generated region starts, which strand it lies on, or how it was divided into six-base tokens. We embed the SynthID tournament watermark in two genomic language models, Carbon and GENERator-v2. The verifier searches both strands, every start position, and four window lengths, and corrects its decision for the whole search. In a detection cohort of 1,544 held-out prompts, it found all 3,088 marked sequences per model, unedited and after one substituted, inserted, or deleted base. For ordinary sequences, the one-sided 95% upper confidence bound on the false-positive rate was at most 0.850%; one bound for the wrong-key control reached 1.015% (Carbon, after a deletion). In a development cohort, neither model showed a detectable change in likelihood or in predefined sequence measures. Under random edits placed without reference to the detector, detection stayed complete or nearly complete up to a 2% per-base edit rate. These results show statistical detection, not biological function, secret-key security, or robustness to an editor who sees the detector. 

## 1 Introduction 

Genomic language models now generate coding sequences, regulatory elements, genome-scale constructs, and func-tionally constrained DNA designs [31, 38, 23]. A DNA sequence does not record whether it came from an organ-ism, a design process, or a generative model, and that provenance is lost once the sequence leaves the system that produced it. One biosecurity proposal for designed proteins is to store synthetic gene sequences and synthesis data in repositories that are queried only in emergencies [3]. A provenance signal carried by the sequence itself would complement such records, because it cannot be separated from the sequence. Watermarking embeds a statistical signal during gener-ation, and a verifier holding the key can later test for it without the model, the prompt, or the generation logs [24]. In DNA, the verifier must first find where to look. Many genomic models write DNA as non-overlapping six-base tokens, but the emitted sequence does not show where one token ends and the next begins. We call the offset of that division the token phase. There are six phases, and the term is unrelated to alignment against a reference genome. Reverse complementation changes the tokens and their order, so both strands must be considered. When the generated region sits inside a longer sequence, its start and end are also unknown. The verifier has to search all of these possibilities and still control the probability of a false positive for the sequence as a whole. We integrate the SynthID tournament watermark [9] into two genomic language models, Carbon-500M [4, 21] and GENERator-v2 1.2B [27, 12]. Both write six-base tokens and can sample directly from the same vocabulary. They are not independent, because the Carbon training corpus is built mainly on GENERator-v2’s pre-training data [4]. SynthID preserves the model’s output distri-bution in expectation over fresh keyed functions, and at a fixed key and context it samples from a deliberately reweighted distribution. We ask whether the sampler follows that distribution, whether watermarking changes likelihood or simple sequence statistics, and whether a ver-ifier can find the mark without knowing the start, strand, or token phase. The study uses two cohorts of prompts with separate roles. A development cohort of 256 prompts serves the sampler check, the quality comparison, and the choice of how to score a window. A detection cohort of 1,608 prompts, of which 1,544 are held out, serves detection, the false-positive rate, and the edit experiments. In both models, the verifier found every marked se-quence in the detection cohort, unedited and after one edited base. For ordinary sequences, every one-sided 95% upper confidence bound on the false-positive rate lay be-low the 1% target. Neither model showed a detectable change in the quality measures. Random edits placed without reference to the detector left detection complete or nearly complete up to a 2% per-base edit rate. After an insertion or deletion, the windows the verifier selected are consistent with regions where the token phase is preserved or recovered. 

## 2 Background 

2.1 Watermarking designed DNA 

DNA watermarks predate generative models. The first synthetic bacterial genome carried watermark sequences [13], and a short signature was written into the genome of an engineered Bacillus subtilis strain [2]. DNA-Crypt hid messages in synonymous codons [18], and messages placed 

1arXiv:2610.04763v1 [q-bio.GN] 3 Oct 2026 in an open reading frame without changing the encoded protein persisted during viral replication [28]. These de-signs need a location for the payload and attention to the biological effect of each change. Generation-time water-marking addresses a complementary setting by embedding the signal through the model’s sampling distribution dur-ing generation, so it needs no payload region. 

2.2 Watermarking language-model output 

Language-model watermarks change how tokens are sam-pled. Some bias a pseudorandom subset of tokens and de-tect its excess frequency [24]. Others leave the output dis-tribution unchanged in expectation over fresh keyed ran-domness [7, 26, 1, 20, 39]. SynthID in its non-distortionary configuration belongs to the second group. We apply it to DNA and propose no new construction. Robustness depends on what the attacker can do, and prior work describes evasion, key-recovery, and forgery attacks by stronger and adaptive attackers [40, 11, 36, 22, 35]. This study tests none of them; its edits are random and never consult the detector. Text watermarks built to survive editing treat synchro-nization as a local problem. Kuditipudi et al. align a candidate text to a key sequence under an edit distance [26], and Kirchenbauer et al. search all contiguous spans and calibrate the false-positive rate for that search [25]. Both rely on an edit disturbing tokenization only near the edit. Golowich and Moitra tolerate a constant fraction of adversarial edits, but with a new construction built on pseudorandom codes [14]. A fixed vocabulary partition keeps the key synchronized under a positional shift, which roughly doubles the provable edit tolerance relative to the soft watermark keyed on the previous token without making the mark immune to edits [42]. Watermarks on sentence embeddings avoid tokenization but need an en-coder and a natural segmentation into units [19], which a DNA verifier lacks. Six-base tokens remove the locality these methods rely on. One inserted or deleted base shifts the reading frame, so every later token changes identity, and so does the keyed context that sets each mark bit. Alignment cannot repair this, because no token sequence survives to be aligned, and a fixed partition does not help, because the changed tokens carry independent mark bits. What survives is a contiguous region read in one of six phases. Verification therefore becomes a search over strands, phases, start positions, and window lengths, and the correction for that search is part of the method. Four properties separate these constructions here: whether verification needs the generating model; whether it needs an alignment, a known boundary, or a known strand; which edits it claims to tolerate; and whether sampling is distorted. Biased-logit schemes distort sam-pling and are usually verified with known boundaries [24]. Distribution-preserving schemes realign to a key sequence [26] or certify a radius of token changes [39]. Watermarks for biological sequence assume a coding region [41] or mark the protein, not its DNA [5]. The construction evaluated here needs no model, alignment, known boundary, or cod-ing region, and it preserves the sampling distribution in expectation. 

2.3 SynthID 

We use the binary tournament construction of SynthID-Text [9]. At each generation step, a secret key and the most recent tokens assign every candidate token a binary mark bit in each tournament layer, and each layer favors candidates whose mark bit is 1. Averaged over fresh keyed functions, the tournament leaves the next-token distribu-tion unchanged. At a fixed key and context, it samples from a reweighted distribution. The detector rebuilds the mark bits from the output and tests for an excess of 1s. Because the bits depend on local context, not on absolute position, they can be rebuilt from any retained stretch of sequence, and the detector needs only the output, the key, and published detector settings (Section 3.3). Keying on recent tokens is common to autoregressive text water-marks [24]. We chose SynthID because it combines local keying with tournament sampling and has been evaluated in a large-scale text deployment. 

2.4 Genomic tokenization and token phase 

Several genomic models, including both studied here, di-vide DNA into non-overlapping six-base tokens, which shortens the token sequence and lets a model cover more bases in a fixed context [8, 38, 4, 27]. Models with one token per base have no phase problem, because an in-sertion or deletion moves tokens without changing them [31, 32]. Caduceus uses one token per base because small input changes can otherwise change the tokenization dras-tically [37]. GENERator randomizes the tokenization offset of each training sample [38], and GENERator-v2 cycles through all six offsets [27]. None of these remedies is available to a verifier that receives only DNA, which must recover the phase from the sequence itself. 

## 3 Methods 

3.1 Models and sampling 

Generation used Carbon-500M [4, 21] and GENERator-v2-eukaryote-1.2b-base [27, 12], two decoder-only models. At every step, we restricted the logits to the 4,096 canonical DNA tokens and normalized once. For GENERator, this is its direct token distribution, not the per-base sampling that its released code applies by default. Temperature was 1.0, with no top-k or top-p truncation. The resulting distribution p is shared by both samplers: the ordinary sampler draws from p without a key, and the marked sampler applies the tournament below. We report each model separately.

3.3 Position-independent detection 

The verifier receives a DNA sequence, the key, and the published detector settings: the generation domain label, the four window lengths, the 30 tournament layers and four-token context used to rebuild the mark bits, the 1,024-context repetition history, and the significance level 

α = 0 .01. In deployment, only the key would be secret. It does not receive the prompt, the model or its probabilities, the seed, the strand, the token phase, or the generation boundary (Figure 1b). The verifier treats every candidate generated region as a window: a stretch of the sequence on one strand, starting at a given base, with one of four lengths. It scores windows at every starting base of the sequence and of its reverse complement, which covers every start position and all six token phases. Inside a window, the DNA is divided into tokens from the window’s first base, and the first four tokens supply context. Every later token whose context is not a repeat is a scored token. 3.4 Prompts and cohorts 

Prompts are 384-base forward-strand windows from 16 accession-versioned RefSeq chromosome records of eight model organisms. Windows were chosen by hashing a fixed label, the accession, and a segment index. Hu-man, patient, controlled-access, organelle, plasmid, and ambiguous-base spans were excluded. Each cohort was fixed before any model generated from it, and no prompt was added, moved, or removed afterward. The development cohort contains 256 prompts. Before any detection result was examined, a hash rule reserved 64 of them for choosing how to summarize the mark bits in a window (Supplementary Section S1) and held out the other 192. The sampler check and the sequence measures use this cohort only. The detection cohort contains 1,608 prompts, 134 non-overlapping windows from each of 12 of the 16 records, covering six of the eight organisms (not mouse or budding yeast), drawn with a separate selection label. The same hash rule set aside 64 prompts, which were not used, and held out 1,544. A separate generation domain label gave both models new keyed functions, and all other settings matched. The same authors generated and scored this cohort with the same code, in a separate computing environment. Every prompt produced two marked and two ordinary continuations of 512 tokens (3,072 bases) per model. The two draws used different seeds, and the two marked draws used different keys, both published for reproducibility. Prepending the prompt to a held-out continuation gives the 3,456-base test sequence that the verifier receives, either a marked sequence or an ordinary sequence; its generation boundary is withheld. Scoring each marked sequence with the other marked draw’s key gives the wrong-key control, which tests whether a positive depends on the correct key. The three rates are reported separately, because a pooled accuracy would depend on how common marked sequences are in an application.

3.5 Sampler validation 

The correctness of the marked sampler rests on implemen-tation tests in the project’s test suite. The tournament update reproduces the public SynthID-Text reference im-plementation [15] on peaked, flat, random, and degenerate distributions. The vectorized computation of pk used dur-ing generation agrees with an independent unvectorized implementation to floating-point precision. Computing 

pk once and applying its inverse-CDF sampling step re-produces the generation sampler’s draws exactly. A second check uses realistic model states. For each model and development prompt, we fixed the state at token index 64 of the first ordinary continuation and drew 5,000 samples from each of p and pk. Each set of counts was compared with its source distribution by the likelihood-ratio ( G) statistic. An expected count of about 1.2 per category is too low for the asymptotic χ2 reference, so G was referred to a Monte Carlo null of 999 samples from the same distribution, which is exact by construction. Because the draws come from the computed distributions, this check confirms the sampling step and the test, not the computation of pk. The 256 tests per sampler form one family under Benjamini–Hochberg correction at 0.05. Bonferroni correction cannot reject here, because 999 replicates allow no P -value below 10 −3, above its level of 1.95 × 10 −4. As a negative control, at eight further states per model, draws from p were tested against pk.

3.6 Sequence measures 

The model score is the teacher-forced negative log-likelihood per generated token under the same model and token restriction as generation, with the prompt as context only. Each model scores its own outputs, so scores are not compared between models. Thirteen fur-ther measures were specified before analysis: GC content; purine content; CpG content; base, dinucleotide, and trin-ucleotide entropy; distinct 6-mer fraction; longest and mean single-base run; Jensen–Shannon drift from the prompt at k = 1 , 2, 3; and perplexity. All 14 measures form one family under Benjamini–Hochberg correction. 

3.7 Edits and statistics 

In the single-edit conditions, each sequence has exactly one substituted, inserted, or deleted base in the continuation, placed by a fixed pseudorandom rule that never consults the verifier. Prompts are the independent unit, and a prompt’s two draws are paired repetitions. Uncertainty in the sequence measures used an equal-weight bootstrap over prompts with 20,000 replicates and a paired sign-flip test with 100,000 replicates. A control prompt is positive if either draw is positive; for marked prompts, we also require both draws to be detected. The detection cohort’s analysis plan, specified before generation, reports the exact one-sided 95% upper confi-dence bound (Clopper–Pearson) on the false-positive rate over the 1,544 held-out prompts. Ordinary sequences, the false positives that matter in use, are the primary control, and the wrong-key control is a secondary check of key specificity. The prompt-level rate is about twice the per-sequence rate that α controls, so comparing it with the 1% target is conservative. Development-cohort rates carry exact two-sided 95% intervals. 

3.8 Edit-rate experiment 

To find where random editing defeats the verifier, we rescored the detection cohort’s stored sequences after applying k = round(3 ,072 r) edits to each continuation. We call r the per-base edit rate and tested seven values from 0.03% to 10% (Figure 5). A substitution changes one base. Each insertion or deletion adds or removes one to five bases, so the edit rate counts edit events, not changed 

4bases. Four series were run: substitutions, insertions only, deletions only, and a mixed series of random insertions and deletions. The single-direction series keep opposite frame shifts from cancelling. Positions and indel lengths came from a public hash stream set by the series, the sequence, and the rate, never by the detector, and no sequence or detector setting changed. Marked sequences were scored with the correct key and ordinary sequences with the corresponding key, 3,088 sequences per model, rate, and series, without wrong-key controls. The analysis plan required the ordinary-sequence rate to stay at or below 1% in every cell, because a rise with edit rate would mean that the edits themselves create apparent signal. 

## 4 Results 

4.1 Sampler fidelity 

At the tested model states, draws from the fixed-key and model distributions agreed with those distributions, and the test could tell the two distributions apart. Each model had 256 tested states, and chance alone predicts 12.8 of them to reject at P < 0.05. The marked sampler produced 13 in Carbon and 5 in GENERator, and the ordinary sampler produced 12 and 14. In GENERator, neither sampler had a rejection after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. For Carbon, the corrected count was not re-tained with the evidence, and its nominal counts are close to chance. The negative control, draws from the model’s distribution tested against the fixed-key distribution, was rejected at all eight control states in each model. Together with the implementation tests (Section 3.5), these results support the correctness of the sampler at the resolution of 5,000 draws per state. They do not prove it correct at every possible state. 

4.2 Sequence-quality proxies 

Watermarking produced no statistically detectable differ-ence between marked and ordinary continuations in either model (Figure 2). Every interval for the 14 measures contained zero, and no comparison was significant after correction. The smallest adjusted P -value was 0.85 in Carbon and 0.73 in GENERator. The paired difference in model score was +0 .00852 nats per token in Carbon and −0.00564 in GENERator. The largest absolute stan-dardized effects were 0.084 standard deviations in Carbon, on the longest single-base run, and 0.111 in GENERator, on the mean single-base run. These results indicate no measurable quality loss under the evaluated proxies. 

4.3 Detection and false-positive rate 

In the detection cohort, the verifier found every marked sequence in both models, unedited and after each single-base edit (Table 1; Figure 3). That is 3,088 sequences per model and condition, and it places the exact two-sided 95% interval for the prompt-level detection rate at 99.76–100%. Every one-sided 95% upper confidence bound on the false-positive rate for ordinary sequences lay below the 1% target. The largest was 0.850%, for GENERator after a deletion. The analysis plan had stated before generation that a bound below 1% required at most eight positive prompts. The wrong-key control, a secondary check of key speci-ficity, crossed 1% in one cell. For Carbon after a deletion, its bound was 1.015%. The correction was conservative in practice. Per se-quence, the corrected decision was positive for 0.105% of ordinary Carbon sequences and 0.186% of ordinary GENERator sequences, pooled across the four conditions, well below α (Supplementary Section S3). The development cohort gave the same result. All 384 marked sequences per model were found in every condition, and each control produced at most one positive sequence per model and condition (Supplementary Table S1). Typical detection strength was the same in both models. The median unedited marked sequence reached strength 792.9 in Carbon and 799.7 in GENERator, far above the threshold. The weakest detected sequences differ more, but in the development cohort that difference came from a single GENERator sequence with a highly repetitive continuation (Supplementary Section S6). 

4.4 Detection after single-base edits 

One inserted or deleted base cost a sequence almost half of its strength, and one substituted base cost little, yet every marked sequence stayed detectable (Figure 4a,c). A substitution lowered the median strength by about 2%, and an insertion or deletion by about 45% (Supplementary Table S2). An insertion or deletion also changed which window car-ried the evidence. For unedited and substituted sequences, the full 3,072-base window was strongest in nearly every sequence. After an insertion or deletion, a shorter window was strongest in almost half of the sequences (Figure 4b,d). A substitution changes token content and nearby contexts but keeps the downstream token phase. An insertion or deletion shifts the division of every later token in a window that spans the edit. The shift toward shorter windows is consistent with the search selecting regions whose token phase is preserved or recovered. The development cohort showed the same shift (Supplementary Figure S2). 

4.5 Detection as edits accumulate 

Detection stayed complete or nearly complete through a 2% per-base edit rate, that is, 61 edits per continuation, in both models (Figure 5). Every marked sequence was found at every rate up to 2% for every edit type, except for one GENERator sequence in the mixed series at 2%. Insertions and deletions defeated the verifier at lower rates than substitutions. At a 5% rate, substitutions were still detected in every Carbon sequence and all but one GENERator sequence, whereas detection after insertions and deletions fell to 92.9–93.9%. At 10%, substitutions were detected in 19.7% of Carbon sequences and 18.7% of GENERator sequences. The other edit types fell to 4.6–5.9%.

## 5 Discussion 

A verifier that knows neither the strand, the start of the generated region, nor the token phase can recover a generation-time watermark from DNA, with one decision corrected for the whole search. This shared watermarking and detection procedure is the methodological contribu-tion. The verifier tolerates random edits because it does not need an intact window. A simple argument predicts failure much earlier: at a 1% rate, stretches free of insertions and deletions average about 100 bases, shorter than the 384-base minimum window. A window, however, gains excess 1s from every token that keeps its phase and context, and the other tokens only dilute the count. An unedited sequence starts near strength 800 against a threshold of 6.21, so it can lose most of its evidence before the decision changes. The shift toward shorter windows after an insertion or deletion fits the same picture, with the search finding the original phase again downstream of the edit. The strongest window does not identify the exact generation boundaries. Running the same sampler and verifier in two mod-els shows that the implementation carries across them. The search addresses a synchronization problem that coding theory treats directly. Synchronization strings add indexing information to recover from insertions and deletions [16], and pseudorandom codes combine indis-tinguishability with decoding under specified errors [6]. Davey and MacKay’s watermark codes integrate over the accumulated drift with a hidden Markov model, but they presume control of the encoding, which a sampling watermark does not have [10]. Our verifier adds no in-dexing code to the sequence. It enumerates the offsets, pays for the search through its correction, and carries none of the error-correction guarantees of those codes. Synonymous-codon watermarks for coding sequences [41] serve a complementary goal, and combining them with generation-time marks would need its own evaluation.

Both cohorts come from the same 16 chromo-some records or a subset of them, which limits extrap-olation to other genomic sources, and longer sequences would increase the search correction. The sampler and quality results come from one execu-tion per model on the development cohort. The detection cohort was generated and scored in a separate computing environment with new keyed functions, but by the same authors with the same code, so it is not an independent replication. For Carbon, the protocol document retained with the development-cohort detection result does not match the hash recorded in that result, and the original document could not be recovered. An audit found that eight of the nine settings it governs are recorded in the re-sult’s own fields and agree with the reported analysis. The ninth, the 1,024-context repetition history, rests only on the default of the detector code identified by its recorded hash. The false-positive evidence is narrow. With 192 prompts, the development cohort could not show a rate below 1% (Supplementary Table S1). In the detection cohort, every bound for ordinary sequences lies below 1%, but the largest, 0.850%, does so narrowly, and the wrong-key bound for Carbon after a deletion exceeds that level at 1.015%. These rates apply to the sequence length and sequence sources tested here; elsewhere, false-positive control rests on the Bonferroni correction alone. Every edit was placed at random, without reference to the detector, and the edit-rate series scored no wrong-key controls. We did not test an adaptive editor who inspects the detector, the key, or any score and places edits where they do the most damage. The keys are published for reproducibility, so secrecy, resistance to key recovery, and removal in deployment were not evaluated, and the analytic null assumes inde-pendent fair mark bits without the correct key. Attacks reported against text watermarks remain untested here, including key recovery from the detector’s P -values [35], paraphrasing and back-translation of SynthID-Text [17], and a layer-inflation attack shown to affect the mean score that our verifier also uses [33]. Model likelihood and sequence statistics are proxies, not measures of expression, replication, function, viability, or safety, and our quality claim covers only the 14 mea-sures. Standardized in-silico suites, such as supervised downstream tasks and zero-shot variant-effect benchmarks [30, 29, 34], score a model’s reading of annotated DNA and mostly rely on labels tied to genome coordinates that a new generation does not carry. Evaluating marked outputs against them would need a paired design or an experimental assay, which is the natural next step before any claim about biological consequence. 

## 7 Conclusion 

The SynthID tournament watermark can be embedded in Carbon-500M and GENERator-v2 1.2B without a statis-tically detectable change in the evaluated quality proxies. A verifier holding the key can recover it from DNA by searching strands, start positions, and window lengths under one correction. In the detection cohort, the verifier found every marked sequence in both models, unedited and after one edited base, and the one-sided 95% upper confidence bound on the false-positive rate for ordinary sequences was at most 0.850%. Detection survived ran-dom edits up to a 2% per-base rate and failed for most 

9sequences at 10%. Broader conclusions require indepen-dent replication, wider sequence coverage, tests against adaptive editing, and biological validation.

S1 Window-score selection 

Before any detection result was examined, 64 develop-ment prompts were reserved for choosing how to sum-marize the mark bits in a window. Neither they nor the 192 held-out prompts were used to fit the detection threshold. The two candidates were the released Syn-thID mean score over scored tokens and the upstream default weighted-mean score. For GENERator, the two were compared on the 64 reserved prompts alone. The comparison used continuations of 384 and 3,072 bases, scored with the token phase known, and measured the separation between marked and ordinary continuations in units of the ordinary-continuation standard deviation. The mean score separated them at least as well at both lengths and was selected. No held-out prompt was scored during this selection, and no signal-matched weights were fitted. We applied the same mean score to Carbon, but the equivalent Carbon comparison is recorded only in the run notes, not as a retained result, so this selection is independently verified for GENERator only. 

S2 Window geometry 

Each scored token carries 30 mark bits, one per tourna-ment layer. The number of bits does not limit detection: 21 bits that are all 1 already reach the corrected threshold, so one scored token would suffice. What limits detection is dilution. The shortest window, 384 bases, holds 64 to-kens. The first four supply unwatermarked context, which leaves at most 60 scored tokens and at most 1,800 mark bits, fewer where a four-token context repeats. With all 60 scored, at least 1,004 of the 1,800 bits must be 1, about 56%. Longer windows need a smaller fraction. Detection therefore depends on recoverable synchronization, not on the absence of edits. 

S3 Conservatism of the correction 

Bonferroni correction bounds the family-wise error rate without requiring independence between tests. Because the searched windows are positively correlated, the effec-tive number of independent tests is smaller than M . The realized false-positive rate can then lie well below α, and recall is attained under a stricter threshold than necessary. The realized per-sequence rates in the detection cohort (Section 4.3) are 9.5 times below α for Carbon and 5.4 times below for GENERator. The observed minimum window P -values behave like those of about 828–852 in-dependent tests, consistently across both models and all four conditions. That is about 19 times fewer than the 16,136 windows searched. This moment-matched figure summarizes how conservative the correction is. It does not replace the decision rule, which is unchanged. 

S4 Search cost and computing environment 

Keyed hashing, not window enumeration, dominates the cost of the search. A sequence of N bases has twelve token frames, two orientations by six phases, and the mark bits of every token position in a frame are computed once, in a single pass. Window statistics then follow from prefix sums over those per-token bit counts, so each window of a given length is evaluated in constant time. Where a frame contains repeated four-token contexts, the first-occurrence rule is enforced with Fenwick trees, and each window costs 

O(log N ) instead. The work is therefore O(N ) keyed hash evaluations and O(N log N ) additional time, with O(N )space per sequence, and M grows linearly in N for a fixed window set. Sequences shorter than every window length are not scored, reverse-complement coordinates are mapped back onto the supplied sequence, and log-probabilities are kept throughout to avoid floating-point underflow. Timing was measured on an AMD EPYC 7763. One 3,456-base sequence took a median 0.0956 s on a single thread. Sequences of 6,912 bases took 0.245 s, and se-quences of 13,824 bases took 0.902 s. Peak memory per process was 27.9 MiB for a 3,456-base sequence. For a 13,824-base sequence, it was 69.1 MiB. Running 16 workers in parallel across sequences gave one sequence per 0.0167 s of wall-clock time. That is 5.7 times the single-threaded rate. Development-cohort generation ran on GPUs. Model scoring ran on CPU for Carbon and on GPU for GENERa-tor. The detection cohort was generated on NVIDIA A100 GPUs in a separate computing environment. Detection ran on x86-64 CPUs for both cohorts.

S6 Strength and the weakest sequence 

Typical strength is nearly the same in both models. In the development cohort, the median unedited marked sequence had strength 790.6 in Carbon and 798.8 in GEN-ERator. Control strengths were concentrated near or below the threshold, and the few positive controls lay only slightly above it (Figure S1a,c). The weakest marked sequence differs more between the models. In the de-velopment cohort, it had strength 124.8 in Carbon and 19.6 in GENERator. In the detection cohort, the weakest sequences were 237.6 in Carbon and 123.8 in GENERator, and the medians were again close (Table S2). The differ-ence between weakest sequences therefore reflects single sequences, not the models’ typical margin. The weakest detected GENERator sequence in the de-velopment cohort contained a highly repetitive continua-tion. Of its four-token contexts, 174 repeated and were excluded from scoring, which left 334 of a possible 508 tokens. Only 54.6% of its mark bits were 1, against a median of 74% in both models. Restoring all 508 scored tokens at that fraction would close only 9% of the gap to Carbon’s weakest sequence, so most of the shortfall comes from weak signal in the tokens that remained. The tournament can raise the fraction of 1s only where the model spreads probability across several tokens, which is consistent with a low-entropy, repetitive stretch carrying little signal. We did not measure per-token entropy to confirm this. We therefore find no evidence that typical detection strength differs between the models. 

S7 Aligned-detector diagnostic in GENERator 

For GENERator, a separate diagnostic checked the ana-lytic null with a detector given the known token phase. Across the four evaluated lengths, 2, 6, 6, and 4 ordinary prompts were positive, against about five expected at each length ( P = 0 .25–0 .87). This result is compatible with the analytic null, but it does not replace the full-search correction used for the position-independent decision.
