# Beyond User-Side Defenses: Image Protection in Open-Weight Diffusion Models 

Jihye Kang Jiwon Park Namhyuk Ahn 

Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Inha University 

# Abstract 

Model providers can support image protection, but open-weight deployment intro-duces practical constraints. We propose four requirements: protection effectiveness, clean fidelity, compatibility, and tamper resistance. We incorporate long-horizon attack simulations to strengthen resistance to protection removal. Experiments with a single protection tool show improved resistance to re-alignment at a cost to clean fidelity, motivating further research toward practical model-side protection. 

# 1 Introduction 

Generative models can imitate artistic styles [1, 2], reproduce personal likenesses [3], and manipulate existing images [4], raising concerns about copyright protection and the misuse of personal images [5]. Image protection methods seek to limit such unauthorized uses by adding small perturbations that interfere with model training or editing [5–7]. These methods enable creators and individuals to take protective measures before sharing their images. However, this approach places the burden of applying protection primarily on users , who must modify their images before publication. Developments in AI regulation, including the EU AI Act, reflect growing attention to copyright and the protection of individual rights in generative AI deployment. Against this background, model providers should also consider how the models they release can support image protection . Recent work has explored this direction by fine-tuning models to disrupt the editing of protected images or resist the personalization of designated subjects [8, 9]. However, demonstrating protective behavior in a model does not by itself establish that the protection is suitable for practical deployment. This raises the question of what requirements model-side image protection should satisfy, particularly when the model is released with open weights and can be modified by downstream users. Hence, we propose four requirements for model-side image protection under open-weight deploy-ment. Protection effectiveness requires the model to inhibit unauthorized use of protected images. 

Clean fidelity requires preserving the model’s behavior on unprotected inputs. Compatibility calls for supporting existing user-side protection tools, allowing users to benefit from model-side protection without adopting a provider-specific procedure. Tamper resistance requires maintaining protection against attempts to remove it by modifying the released weights. These requirements connect the protection needs of users with the constraints of model deployment. They provide a basis for assessing the benefits of model-side protection and the trade-offs involved in implementing it. Our technical contribution focuses on tamper resistance while accounting for protection effectiveness and clean fidelity. We study a re-alignment attack that fine-tunes the released model to reconstruct protected inputs as their clean counterparts, thereby suppressing its protective behavior. To resist this attack, we train the model to retain protection after simulated weight updates. The training incorporates longer attack trajectories to account for changes that a short inner loop alone fails to capture. Experiments with a single protection tool show improved resistance to re-alignment compared with protective alignment alone and short-horizon training, while revealing a trade-off with clean fidelity. How to extend this resistance across existing protection tools while preserving ordinary model utility remains an open question for practical model-side image protection.

# 2 Related Work 

User-side image protection. Image protection methods add imperceptible perturbations to an image so that a diffusion model fails to edit or personalize it faithfully [10, 11, 5, 7, 6]. MetaCloak uses meta-learning to keep such perturbations effective across surrogate models and data transforma-tions [12]. Impasto improves their invisibility [13], and FastProtect reduces the generation cost through perturbation pre-training [14]. These methods assume a fixed model. Purification can weaken the perturbation without modifying the model [15, 16]. CAT instead adapts the latent autoencoder with lightweight adapters [17] and neutralizes existing protective perturbations [18], which suggests that a user-side defense offers limited guarantees once an attacker controls the weights. 

Model-side image protection. Recent work moves protection into the model itself [8, 9]. GuardDoor fine-tunes the autoencoder of a diffusion model so that protected inputs reconstruct to a designated pattern and cannot be edited faithfully [8]. APDM updates a diffusion model to block the personal-ization of designated subjects [9]. Our protective alignment follows the autoencoder-based design. We study whether such protection persists when the released weights are modified to remove it. 

Resistance to fine-tuning. Outside image protection, self-destructing models [19] and TAR [20] train models against simulated fine-tuning attacks so that adapting them to harmful tasks becomes costly. For text-to-image diffusion models, IMMA [21] and GIFT [22] immunize models against malicious adaptation, and SleeperMark [23] embeds watermarks that persist after fine-tuning. Model-side image protection faces the same problem. There, the property to retain is the response to protected inputs. 

# 3 Deployment Scenario 

We consider a model provider that releases an open-weight diffusion model and users who wish to protect their images from unauthorized editing or personalization. Users apply an image protection tool before publishing their images. The provider aligns the model to support the protection proce-dure, aiming to strengthen protection on protected inputs while preserving the model’s behavior on unprotected inputs. The resulting model weights are released for downstream use. 

3.1 Threat Model 

The released model processes both protected and unprotected images, and downstream users have access to its weights. A malicious user may use the model to edit or personalize protected images and may modify the weights to circumvent the protection. The attacker holds the released weights and the public protection tool, and can therefore construct clean and protected image pairs from auxiliary images. We assume that the attacker cannot obtain unprotected weights of the components the provider modifies, since replacing them would otherwise restore the original model without any training. This corresponds to a provider that releases the model only in its protected form. Our experiments use a public base model as a testbed for this setting. Among removal attacks, we consider the re-alignment attack . It reruns protective alignment after replacing the protective target with the 2clean image for each protected input. Its cost is paid once and amortized across images, whereas purification [15, 16] must be applied to each protected image. Training a replacement model from scratch is outside our scope. 

3.2 Model-Side Protection Requirements 

We discuss the requirements that model-side image protection should satisfy for practical open-weight deployment. A model’s ability to protect designated images must be considered alongside its support for existing protection tools, its behavior on ordinary inputs, and its resistance to protection removal. We therefore propose the following four requirements. 

Protection effectiveness. The model should inhibit unauthorized editing or personalization of images processed by a supported protection tool. Evaluation should measure protection on the downstream task of interest, since a change in the model’s intermediate representation or reconstruction does not necessarily imply that the intended misuse has been prevented. 

Clean fidelity. The model should preserve its behavior on unprotected inputs. Because the same weights process both protected and unprotected images, alignment can affect ordinary reconstruction and generation. These effects should be evaluated alongside protection effectiveness to determine the cost of introducing protection into the model. 

Compatibility. Model-side protection should support existing user-side protection tools, reducing the need for users to adopt provider-specific procedures or reprocess previously published images. Evaluation should specify which tools are supported and distinguish support for tools used during alignment from generalization to tools not seen during training. 

Tamper resistance. Protection should persist under attempts to remove it through modification of the released weights. This requirement must be assessed under a specified attack scope and computational budget. Evaluating protection after such modifications is necessary to determine whether the protective behavior survives downstream adaptation. 

# 4 Method 

In this paper, we focus on tamper resistance while accounting for protection effectiveness and clean fidelity. We study this problem using a single protection tool, aligning the model with the protection procedure and training it to retain the protective behavior under model modification. Let x denote a clean image, ˜x its protected version, and T its assigned target pattern. We denote the autoencoder parameters by θ and use the original frozen encoder and decoder, Eg and Dg , as references [24]. 

4.1 Protective Alignment 

The provider aligns the autoencoder so that protected inputs reconstruct to designated high-contrast patterns while unprotected inputs retain their original behavior. The encoder, quantization convolu-tions, and decoder tail are optimized with 

Lalign = Lprot (˜ x, T ) + Lfid (x). (1) The protection loss Lprot encourages the reconstruction of ˜x to match T in image space and its encoding to match Eg (T ) in latent space. The fidelity loss Lfid preserves clean reconstructions and latent features using x and the frozen autoencoder as references. Targets are generated separately for each image and share a high-contrast structure. Appendix A provides the full loss definitions. 

4.2 Tamper-Resistance Training 

The re-alignment attack of Section 3.1 replaces the protective target T in Equation 1 with the clean image x. The evaluated attack updates the encoder to suppress the protective behavior and restore reconstruction of protected inputs. To resist such modifications, we optimize the aligned model to retain protection after simulated attacks using first-order meta-learning [25]. Prior work has explored training model safeguards to resist harmful adaptation [19, 20]. We investigate this direction for image protection, focusing on the effect of the simulated attack horizon. # 5 Experimental Results 

Experimental setup. We use Stable Diffusion 2.1 [24] with LPIPS-constrained Mist [11] at ϵ =2/255 . Training uses 3,602 WikiArt images [26], re-alignment uses 73 disjoint Monet paintings, and evaluation uses 100 held-out WikiArt images. Protection is measured by LPIPS [27] between SDEdit [28] outputs from protected and clean inputs under identical sampling conditions. Clean fidelity is measured by reconstruction PSNR on unprotected inputs relative to the original autoencoder. We evaluate encoder re-alignment over 20 attack epochs. Appendix B provides the full protocol. 4Comparison with baselines. Table 1 compares protective alignment alone with short- and long-horizon tamper-resistance training. All three models provide similar protection before the attack. The two training variants differ only in the epoch-long refresh, isolating the contribution of the extended simulation. However, re-alignment reduces the protection scores of alignment alone and short-horizon training below the unaligned reference within one attack epoch. Figure 3 shows that neither regains protection in the remaining epochs, whereas long-horizon training retains a score of 0.714 after one epoch and 0.724 after ten. Long-horizon training retains a score of 0.622 after the full attack budget, compared with 0.118 and 0.107 for the two baselines. The attack duration needed to reach the removal threshold increases from one epoch to beyond the 20-epoch evaluation window. Figure 1 illustrates the corresponding differences in editing outputs. The improvement therefore concerns the persistence of protection under attack, rather than stronger protection at release. 

Analysis. Figure 2 examines how the simulation horizon affects agreement with the evaluation attack. The directional agreement of short simulations decreases as the attack progresses, whereas the longer simulation remains more closely aligned with later attack updates. This supports incorporating extended attack trajectories when training the model to retain protection under weight modification. Additional analysis of displacement directions and magnitudes is provided in Appendix C.1. Improved resistance comes with a clean-fidelity cost. Long-horizon training achieves 25.7 dB, compared with 29.8 dB for short-horizon training and 30.9 dB for alignment alone. Removing the separation loss Lgap improves clean fidelity by only 0.3 dB but reduces the post-attack protection score from 0.622 to 0.161. Ablations in Appendix C.3 further show that removing clean-fidelity or preservation losses lowers clean fidelity even when the protection score remains above the unaligned reference. These results emphasize the need to assess retained protection alongside ordinary model behavior. Appendix C.4 provides further analysis of this trade-off. 

# 6 Concluding Remarks 

The observed clean-fidelity cost raises a design question: how can protective alignment resist removal while limiting changes to ordinary model behavior? The choice of alignment targets and trainable parameters offers concrete directions for investigating this trade-off. Future work should examine whether protection can be concentrated in model components that respond to protected inputs without substantially affecting unprotected ones. Compatibility broadens this question beyond a single protection procedure. Users may have already published images processed by different tools, making provider-specific reprocessing impractical. Supporting these images calls for studying both alignment with multiple existing tools and gener-alization to tools not seen during training. For open-weight deployment, these extensions should also be evaluated under a broader range of weight-modification attacks and computational budgets. Understanding how tool coverage, clean fidelity, and attack resistance interact will help guide the deployment of model-side image protection. 

Limitations. The evaluated attack updates only the encoder, although the defense also modifies the decoder tail, and no attack is tuned against the defense itself. Because the protection score measures the distance between edits, it cannot tell a triggered pattern from an edit degraded into texture, which the long-horizon model produces past t = 20 . The effect also varies: a second run retains 0.495 instead of 0.622, and one of the 100 images is never protected. Appendix D gives the full list. 

Broader Impact. The defense we study aims to let people who publish images resist unauthorized editing even after the model is released in open weights. Our position is that failing to evaluate tamper resistance overstates what such a defense delivers, and a user who acts on an overstated guarantee is worse off than one who knows the protection is fragile. That applies to our own result: raising the attacker’s cost from one epoch to more than twenty is a delay on a single consumer GPU, not security. The re-alignment attack we describe targets protections that are already published, but it is the provider’s own alignment procedure run with a different target and needs no capability an infringer lacks. Tamper resistance is dual-use: it is desirable when the retained property is a protection and undesirable when it is a backdoor, a bias, or a restriction a downstream user has a legitimate reason to remove. Our method also costs clean fidelity for every user of the released model, including those who never asked for protection. We evaluate on published artworks rather than on personal images, we redistribute none of them, and we introduce no new dataset. Appendix E gives the full statement. 5Acknowledgments. The implementation additionally computes margin quantities between the target, anchor and clean distances, and a second hinge in latent space; these are logged for diagnosis and are not part of the objective. Both horizons take first-order inner steps. The inner gradients are detached, so the outer gradient reaches the parameters the simulated attack did not touch exactly, as if no inner loop had run, and the attacked ones through the identity map ∂ψ S /∂θ S = I. The offset ∆ is detached for the same reason, so no gradient is propagated through the epoch-long attack and an outer step still costs K inner steps. It is recomputed every epoch because the defense moves during training and a stale offset ceases to describe the current model. Algorithm 1 states the procedure and Table 2 lists every constant. 

A.4 Hyperparameters A.5 Training Cost 

Protective alignment and tamper-resistance training use a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090. Protective alignment takes about 35 hours for 80 epochs and tamper-resistance training about 36 hours for 20, so an epoch of tamper-resistance training costs roughly four times an epoch of protective alignment. Each outer step includes K simulated attack updates, and epochs with K = 5 take about a third longer than those with K = 3 . Of the 36 hours of tamper-resistance training, the once-per-epoch refresh accounts for 6.5 and the outer loop for the rest. Evaluating one model costs a fifty-epoch attack and an SDEdit sweep at every recorded epoch, and the ablations and the checkpoint sweep of 9Appendix C.3 and Appendix C.4 repeat that twelve times. The numbers above exclude the exploratory runs that preceded them. 

# B Experimental Setup 

B.1 Evaluation Scope 

Training a replacement model from scratch on the deployed model’s outputs lies outside our scope, since no weight-level defense can withstand it in principle. Compatibility with existing user-side protection tools is one of the requirements proposed in Section 3. Our experiments consider a single protection tool applied by users, with the provider aligning the model to support that tool. Compatibility across multiple protection tools remains an open question. Among the removal attacks available, re-alignment is the cheapest because it is paid once rather than per image. Purification runs on every protected image the attacker wants to use and gives up some image quality each time [15, 16], and searching for an input the alignment misses is per-image as well. Re-alignment uses the protection tool and the released checkpoint in a single fine-tuning run, after which the modified model can be reused across images. 

B.2 Datasets and Attack Configuration 

Every model starts from stabilityai/stable-diffusion-2-1-base and every image is pro-cessed at 512 × 512 . Protective alignment and tamper-resistance training use 3,602 WikiArt images; the attack runs on 73 Monet paintings, disjoint from that set; protection effectiveness and clean fidelity are measured on WikiArt images held out from training, drawn as a 200-image pool sampled with the training set excluded by normalized filename and balanced by artist, of which an evenly strided half is evaluated. The WikiArt images come from the refined WikiArt collection of Tan et al. [26], ob-tained through the public re-upload at https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/steubk/wikiart ,and are used for non-commercial research only. All sets are protected with the same tool and budget, LPIPS-constrained Mist [11] at ϵ = 2 /255 in fused mode, with the per-image target described in Section 4. The attack descends the provider’s own Lalign with the original image in place of the target and the target mask disabled, since a mask derived from the pattern is meaningless once the pattern is the clean image. It trains the encoder with AdamW at a learning rate of 10 −5, keeping the weights of Table 2, and we run it for fifty epochs and use the first twenty for the primary comparisons. 

B.3 Protection Effectiveness 

We measure protection effectiveness by comparing the edit of a protected image against the edit of its clean counterpart under the same model, prompt, strength, guidance and seed, so the two outputs differ only through the autoencoder. Edits use SDEdit [28] with the prompt a painting , strength 0.5, guidance 7.5, 50 steps and a single fixed seed. A protection that activates drives the two apart, so higher LPIPS [27] and lower SSIM [29] indicate stronger protection. Every value we report is a mean over the 100 evaluation images; the standard deviation of protection LPIPS across them is 0.063 without alignment, 0.068 after protective alignment and 0.081 after tamper-resistance training. We do not report FID, since the evaluation set is too small for it to be meaningful. 

B.4 Clean Fidelity 

Clean fidelity is measured without diffusion, as the reconstruction of an unprotected image compared against the reconstruction of the frozen original autoencoder, in PSNR and LPIPS. We measure the reconstruction rather than a full editing pipeline because the alignment changes only the autoencoder, which every editing and personalization path passes through, so its reconstruction error is what those paths inherit. This isolates the effects of protective alignment and tamper-resistance training on unprotected inputs, following the reconstruction comparison used in prior work [8]. Measured against the original image instead, the original autoencoder reaches 27.2 dB, the aligned model 27.7 dB and the long-horizon model 24.7 dB. Alignment is slightly above the original model on that axis because both training procedures use the same painting domain the evaluation images come from, which is a domain effect and not evidence that the defense is free. B.5 Tamper Resistance 

We measure tamper resistance by the first attack epoch at which protection LPIPS falls to or below the unaligned reference of 0.225. The same definition is used in Table 1, Table 5, and Table 6. A removal epoch of > 20 indicates that this threshold was not reached within the first 20 attack epochs. A model whose protection has been removed falls below that level rather than merely reaching it: the attacked alignment settles at 0.118. We attribute the gap to the attack neutralizing the perturbation the protection tool applied along with the alignment itself, and we did not test that attribution separately. 

B.6 Imperceptibility of Protected Images 

The protection budget is ϵ = 2 /255 in pixel space with an LPIPS constraint. Measured distortion between protected and original images is 45.1 dB PSNR, 0.990 SSIM and 0.010 LPIPS on the same 100 images used to evaluate protection effectiveness and clean fidelity. Imperceptibility is a property of the protection tool we adopt, since our trigger is that tool’s output at the budget above. At this budget the trigger is far below the distortion either training procedure introduces, so the measured protection is not bought with visible damage to the published image.

# D Limitations 

The attack configuration is fixed rather than searched: we evaluate one removal attack, on one scope, at one learning rate. On the training side the schedule of Table 2 reaches only the first twenty of its thirty-six configurations in a twenty-epoch run, so the combined encoder-and-decoder-tail scope is never simulated. The protection relies on an image-space perturbation, so resistance to purification and compatibility across multiple user-side protection tools remain open questions. Every number here is at a single protection budget, ϵ = 2 /255 , which is also the budget our trigger uses; whether the 14 exchange holds at other budgets is untested, as is whether it holds for a base model other than Stable Diffusion 2.1 or a protection tool other than Mist. Appendix C.4 maps the trade-off along one training run, and we did not sweep a deliberate control such as the weight λ or the number of inner steps. All values are means over 100 images from a single sampling seed, and the two runs of Appendix C.2 are the only evidence we have on run-to-run variation. The defense is also not uniform across images. On one of the 100 evaluation images both training procedures fail: the aligned model reconstructs it at 7.0 dB against the original autoencoder, and after tamper-resistance training the protection does not activate on it at all, protection LPIPS reaching 0.21 against the 0.225 an unaligned model already gives. The means we report absorb that case rather than excluding it. Past t = 20 the long-horizon model stops reproducing the designated pattern and the edit degrades into unstructured texture, so imitation still fails but the distance-based protection score cannot distinguish the two cases. Two directions follow. The clean-fidelity cost is the quantity most worth reducing, and two design choices are the natural candidates: training the encoder alone instead of the encoder and the decoder tail, and choosing a target the encoder can satisfy in latent space instead of one the decoder must render. Separately, Table 3 shows the refresh tracks the attacker more closely when it descends the re-alignment objective than when it descends Equation 4, which the trained models use; refreshing with the former is an improvement we have measured but not yet trained with. 

# E Broader Impact 

The defense we study is meant to give people who publish images a way to resist unauthorized editing that does not stop working the moment the model is released in open weights. Our position is that failing to evaluate tamper resistance overstates what such a defense delivers, and a user who acts on an overstated guarantee is worse off than one who knows the protection is fragile. That applies to our own result as much as to anyone’s: raising the attacker’s cost from one epoch to more than twenty is a delay on a single consumer GPU, not security, and no one should publish work on the strength of it that they could not afford to see edited. We also describe an attack against protections that are already published, which is a disclosure with its own cost. We judge that cost small, since the attack is the provider’s own alignment procedure run again with a different target and needs no capability an infringer does not already have. Tamper resistance has a dual-use face. It makes a property of released weights hard to remove by fine-tuning, and that is desirable when the property is a protection and undesirable when it is a backdoor, a bias or a restriction a downstream user has a legitimate reason to remove. Our method also costs clean fidelity for every user of the released model, including those who never asked for protection, which is why clean fidelity is reported at the same checkpoint rather than separately.
