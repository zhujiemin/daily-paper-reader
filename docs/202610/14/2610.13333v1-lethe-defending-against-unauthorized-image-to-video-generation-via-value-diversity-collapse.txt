ABSTRACT
Image-to-video (I2V) diffusion models can animate a single reference image into
high-quality, subject-consistent videos, posing threats of misuse as anyone can
turn personal photos into fabricated videos without consent. A promising solution
is to proactively add an adversarial perturbation to the image before release, such
that videos generated from it would be unfaithful or visually corrupted. However,
due to an insufficient understanding of what governs how subsequent frames read
the reference image in modern I2V models, existing protections struggle to offer
strong protection under common perturbation budgets. In this paper, we approach
the problem by exploring this underlying reading mechanism. Through a convex
geometric analysis, we theoretically identify the spread of the reference value vec-
tors as a key factor governing this reading process, and empirically find that even a
mild contraction of this spread can severely degrade the generated video, making
it an efficient lever for protection. Guided by this insight, we propose LETHE, a
novel protection method that directly disrupts this reading process. LETHE pairs
two complementary objectives, where a value collapse objective drives the ref-
erence value vectors toward uniformity, while an attention attraction objective
draws subsequent frames toward these collapsed tokens to amplify corruption.
We further construct two human-centric I2V protection benchmarks and conduct
extensive experiments with multiple modern I2V models, which demonstrate the
state-of-the-art protection performance and versatility of LETHE.

INTRODUCTION
Image-to-video (I2V) diffusion models (Yang et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2025) have achieved remark-
able progress in video synthesis. Given a reference image (e.g., a selfie) and a text prompt, these
models can generate a high-quality video in which the subject faithfully retains the appearance in
the reference image while performing the actions described in the prompt (Huang et al., 2024).
However, this capability also enables malicious individuals to animate a personal photo shared on-
line into videos without the owner’s consent, such as videos that fabricate the person’s words and
actions. Since such videos depict the person in natural motion, they can be deceptively realistic
and thus pose serious threats to personal privacy and reputation, which have drawn growing public
concern and legislative attention (Gui et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026; Kamachee et al., 2025).
To mitigate this threat, one emerging solution is protective perturbation (Zhang et al., 2026), which
turns the vulnerability of deep learning models to adversarial perturbations (Szegedy et al., 2013)
into a means of protecting personal images. The key idea is to proactively add a small, carefully
crafted perturbation to the photo before sharing it online, so that diffusion models taking the pro-
tected photo as input are misled into producing unfaithful or low-quality outputs (Salman et al.,
2023; Liang et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2024; Gui et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026). In this way, forgeries
could be blocked at the source, substantially reducing the potential harm from their spread.
While protective perturbations have achieved notable success in image editing and customiza-
tion (Liang et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2024; Salman et al., 2023), protection against I2V generation

need for stronger protection tailored to modern attention-based I2V models.
To approach this problem, in this paper, we first seek to understand the key factor that governs how
I2V models read information from the reference image. In modern I2V models, the reference image
is encoded into a set of tokens, from which subsequent frames read primarily through attention op-
erations (Liu et al., 2026). Characterizing this reading process from a convex geometric perspective,
we theoretically identify the spread of the reference value vectors, measured by the radius of their
minimum enclosing ball, as an upper bound on the diversity of the reference readouts. This analysis
points to reference-value diversity as a key target towards disrupting the reading process. We further
empirically find that even a mild contraction of this spread suffices to severely degrade the gener-
ated video, making it an efficient lever for protection. Motivated by this insight, we propose LETHE,
which crafts protective perturbation by disrupting this reading process. LETHE pairs two comple-
mentary objectives, where the value collapse objective drives the reference value vectors toward
uniformity, aiming to tighten the above bound, while another attention attraction objective draws
the attention of subsequent frames toward these collapsed tokens to amplify disruption. In this way,
LETHE constrains both the content and the source of the information read by subsequent frames, so
that the protected photo can hardly be faithfully animated into a high-quality video. Lastly, since
a standardized and publicly available benchmark tailored to human-centric I2V protection is still
largely lacking, we construct two high-quality I2V protection benchmarks by re-annotating CelebV-
Text (Yu et al., 2023) and Kinetics-700 (Carreira et al., 2019) with carefully curated reference frames
and human-verified motion prompts. Extensive experiments on these benchmarks with multiple I2V
models confirm the state-of-the-art effectiveness and versatility of LETHE.
In conclusion, our contributions are three-fold:
• We characterize the reference-reading pathway in attention-based I2V models from a convex geo-
metric perspective and identify reference-value diversity as a key quantity that bounds the diversity
of the reference readouts. Combining this analysis with direct value interventions, we establish
reference-value diversity as a useful and efficient target for protective perturbations.
• We propose LETHE, a novel protection method that safeguards images against unauthorized I2V,
by directly disrupting how these models read the reference image. With a pair of complemen-
tary objectives, LETHE jointly constrains the content and the source of the information read by
subsequent frames, thereby effectively preventing them from reproducing the reference image.

RELATED WORK
Image-to-Video Diffusion Models. Given a reference image and a prompt, I2V diffusion mod-
els aim to generate videos that preserve the appearance of the reference image while following the
motion described by the prompt, and they have been widely adopted in content creation and anima-
tion (Wang et al., 2026). Early I2V models, such as SVD (Blattmann et al., 2023), are mostly built
upon UNet architectures extended from text-to-image models. Constrained by their model capacity
and spatiotemporal modeling ability, these models remain limited in generation fidelity and tempo-
ral consistency (Wang et al., 2026). More recent models, such as CogVideoX (Yang et al., 2025),
Wan (Wan et al., 2025), and LTX-Video (HaCohen et al., 2024), shift to DiT architectures with full
spatiotemporal attention, substantially improving subject fidelity, generation quality, and temporal
coherence. These models typically encode the reference image into latent tokens aligned with the
first frame, and subsequent frames primarily interact with these tokens through attention to acquire
visual information. This reading pathway plays a key role in video generation, as evidenced by
recent long video generation methods (Liu et al., 2026; Yi et al., 2025), which demonstrate that re-
taining the first-frame tokens as attention anchors can help improve long-range subject consistency.
In this work, we show that this reading pathway also offers an efficient lever for protection.
Protective Perturbations against Unauthorized Visual Generation. While generative models
have greatly facilitated visual content creation, they are also increasingly misused, e.g., for unau-
thorized style mimicry and identity forgery (Zhang et al., 2026). To prevent such misuse, protective
perturbations add small adversarial noise to images before release, aiming to block unauthorized
generation at the source. In the era of diffusion models, PhotoGuard (Salman et al., 2023) and
AdvDM (Liang et al., 2023) are among the earliest to protect images from being edited and cus-
tomized. These methods typically perturb the image’s VAE latent or maximize the denoising loss,
so that models like Stable Diffusion cannot faithfully process the protected images. To date, this re-
search area remains highly active, with subsequent studies further improving efficiency (Xue et al.,
2024) and enhancing protection in specific scenarios (Li et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2026).
With the rapid advancement of I2V models and the growing concerns over their misuse, recent
studies have begun to extend protective perturbations to I2V generation (Gui et al., 2025; Song et al.,
2026). Early efforts mainly target UNet-based models (Pang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025). Since
these models largely inherit their components from image diffusion models, these methods naturally
build upon designs from image protection, e.g., disrupting the encoders. As DiT-based models
become the mainstream, more recent studies further introduce objectives on intermediate features or
the denoising process (Long et al., 2026; Song et al., 2026), demonstrating the promise on modern
I2V models. However, designing such objectives is inherently challenging: in DiT-based models, the
reference image’s features propagate to subsequent frames through deeply stacked spatiotemporal
attention, and it remains largely unclear which internal factor governs how much of the reference is
reproduced. Without such an understanding, protection objectives have to be defined on proxies that
are only indirectly related to this reading process, and part of the budget may be spent on features that
contribute little to what subsequent frames read, limiting the defenses’ effectiveness. In this paper,
we aim to fill this gap by examining how I2V models read the reference image, which allows us to
concentrate the budget more directly on the reading process and unlock more effective protection.

MOTIVATING ANALYSIS
As discussed in Sec. 2, understanding how subsequent frames read from the reference image is
central towards effective protection. In this section, we first formalize this reading process in modern
I2V models and then analyze the factors influencing it, which motivates the design of our protection.
The Reading Pathway. Given a reference image xref and a text prompt y, I2V models such as
Wan 2.1 and CogVideoX generate a video by starting from Gaussian noise zT and iteratively denois-
ing it. At each step, the current latent zt is split into spatio-temporal patches and linearly projected

tokens at specific positions (typically those of the first frame), which we refer to as reference tokens.
Inside the denoising network, the MLP and the normalization layers act on each token separately,
and attention is the main operator that moves information between tokens (Yang et al., 2025; Wan
et al., 2025). Concretely, within one head of one layer, each reference token i is projected into a
key vector ki = WKhi and a value vector vi = WV hi, where hi is its hidden state. The key serves

that are transferred. In turn, a subsequent-frame token is projected into a query qm = WQhm,
which is matched against the keys, and the token receives the values in proportion to the match.
For instance, when xref depicts a person, the query of the token generating the face in a subsequent
frame matches the keys of the facial tokens, and it then receives the facial values, while the token
generating the background likewise receives the background values. In this way, each position
obtains the content it needs, and the video reproduces the visual features of xref. Formally, let om

METHODOLOGY
Problem Formulation. We formulate the problem as follows. An image owner (the defender) wants
to publish an image xref (e.g., a selfie), and does not want it to be used as the reference image of an
I2V model to fabricate videos. To this end, the defender releases a protected image x′ = xref + δ.
Following prior work on protective perturbations (Gui et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026), we adopt the
commonly used ℓ∞budget with ∥δ∥∞≤ϵ. Then, an attacker may collect x′ from the Internet
and feed it, possibly after input transformations, into an I2V model with an arbitrary prompt y and
random noise zT . The defender’s goal is to craft the protective perturbation δ such that the generated
video can no longer faithfully reproduce xref with high quality.
Value Collapse Objective. Proposition 1 identifies the spread of the reference values as an up-
per bound on reference-readout diversity, while our intervention experiment shows that contracting
these values can substantially disrupt generation. This motivates us to disrupt video generation by
collapsing the values of the reference tokens. However, R(V ) is inconvenient to optimize directly:
computing it requires solving a minimum enclosing ball problem at every forward pass, and its gra-
dient is sparse, being nonzero only for the few values lying on the boundary. Moreover, the absolute
scale of R(V ) varies across layers and heads, which makes it hard to be aggregated.

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Models. We mainly conduct our experiments on 2 SOTA native image-to-video generative models,
i.e., Wan2.1-I2V-14B (Wan et al., 2025) and CogVideoX-5B-I2V (Yang et al., 2025), which repre-
sent the mainstream DiT-based paradigm with distinct training objectives and attention designs. To
evaluate transferability, we also evaluate on LTX-Video (HaCohen et al., 2024), SVD (Blattmann
et al., 2023), LightX2V (LightX2V Team, 2026), and AccVideo (Zhang et al., 2025), covering mod-
els with completely different architectures and training data, as well as popular community models.
Baselines. We compare LETHE with 5 representative baselines that protect images against diffusion
models, including PhotoGuard (Salman et al., 2023), AdvDM (Liang et al., 2023), SDS+ (Xue
et al., 2024), I2VGuard (Gui et al., 2025), and Anti-Prompt (Song et al., 2026), where Anti-Prompt
is a very recent work published in 2026 and represents the state of the art that specifically targets
disrupting I2V generation. All baselines are reproduced following their original papers and official
code (if open sourced) with recommended hyperparameters. To ensure fairness, all methods share
the same perturbation budget (ϵ = 16/255 by default) and inference configurations.

et al., 2019) carry annotations designed for other tasks (e.g., attribute descriptions or action labels)
that cannot directly serve as I2V prompts. Therefore, we construct and annotate two evaluation

Metrics. We evaluate protection performance from both identity preservation and generation qual-
ity perspectives. For identity preservation, we report the Identity Score Matching (ISM, ↓) (Van Le

which further accounts for video-level quality (e.g., temporal consistency and motion naturalness).
Lower ISM, QAF, and QAV indicate that the generated video preserves less identity and has lower
visual quality, and thus a stronger protection. Additionally, following Song et al. (2026), we adopt
an MLLM-based evaluation protocol as a complement to the above metrics.

5.3
TRANSFERABILITY & ROBUSTNESS
Transferability. In practice, an attacker may not use exactly the same model as the surrogate. To
account for this, we further evaluate whether the perturbations crafted on Wan2.1 transfer to models
that are not directly involved in the optimization, and compare LETHE with Anti-Prompt. Fig. 5(a)
reports the relative reduction of each metric with respect to the clean images. On LightX2V and
AccVideo, which are built on the same DiT architecture as Wan2.1, LETHE retains most of its effec-
tiveness (e.g., lowering all metrics by ∼40%). On SVD and LTX-Video, which differ substantially
from Wan2.1 in architecture, LETHE still yields clear degradation (e.g., ∼30% ISM reduction for
both models) and consistently outperforms Anti-Prompt on all four metrics. We hypothesize that
this is because tokens carrying reference information are also aggregated through softmax attention
in all these models, so the reduced diversity of the reference representation induced by LETHE also
generalizes beyond the surrogate. We leave a more thorough investigation to future work.

formations (e.g., enhancement by platform), deliberate adversarial purification by the attacker, and
generation with prompts and seeds unknown to the defender. To test LETHE’s robustness under these
conditions, following Vu et al. (2026), we evaluate three groups of settings on Wan2.1: (I) 4 common

5.4
ABLATION STUDIES
Effect of each objective. We first ablate the two objectives. As shown in Tab. 2, using Lvc alone
already provides strong protection, e.g., reducing ISM-A from 0.817 to 0.590 and QAF from 0.912
to 0.522 on CelebV-Text, respectively. Adding Laa further amplifies the protection by a notable
margin, as reflected by consistently lower values on all four metrics across both datasets. This is
consistent with our analysis in Sec. 4 that Lvc primarily collapses the convex hull, whereas Laa
keeps the attention on these tokens, which amplifies the collapse and makes the protection stronger.
Effect of the budget. Fig. 6 shows how each metric varies with the perturbation budget ϵ. Consistent
with the common observation in this field (Liang et al., 2023; Long et al., 2026), a larger budget
yields stronger protection, and all four metrics decrease monotonically with ϵ. Notably, with only
half of the perturbation budget (ϵ = 8/255), LETHE already achieves protection comparable to
several strong baselines at ϵ = 16/255, e.g., an ISM-A of 0.58 and a QAV of 0.69. This suggests
that LETHE spends the perturbation budget more efficiently, as both of its objectives directly target
the pathway through which subsequent frames read the reference image.
6
CONCLUSION
This paper studies how to protect personal images against unauthorized I2V generation. We first
show that subsequent frames read visual content from the reference frame primarily with attention,
and demonstrate through a convex hull-based view that the diversity of these readouts is bounded
by the spread of the reference values. Our intervention experiment further confirms that contracting
this spread effectively disrupts generation. Building on this analysis, we propose LETHE, which
consists of a value collapse loss that suppresses the diversity of the reference values and an attention
attraction loss that draws the attention of subsequent frames to the collapsed reference tokens, so
that the protected images can no longer be faithfully animated. Extensive experiments on multiple
I2V models and two re-annotated human-centric datasets demonstrate LETHE’s strong performance
in various settings. We hope LETHE, together with our analyses and the dataset can deepen the
community’s understanding and inspire more effective image protection methods in the future.

ETHICS STATEMENT
This work is motivated by the growing concern that personal images can be used without authoriza-
tion to generate videos of the depicted person through image-to-video models. By analyzing how
these models read visual content from the reference image, we hope to provide insights into what
governs the effectiveness of protective perturbations in this setting, and we believe LETHE repre-
sents a valuable step toward more effective protection of personal images. All models used in this
work are publicly released and used in accordance with their licenses. Our evaluation sets are built
upon CelebV-Text and Kinetics-700, which we use for non-commercial research in accordance with
their terms of use. Our re-annotation only adds textual descriptions and collects no new personal
data. To comply with the original terms, we will release only the textual annotations together with
the video identifiers and frame indices, so that users obtain the original data from official sources.
The qualitative examples in this paper are limited to those needed to illustrate the protection effect.
