## Abstract 

The rapid spread of face forgeries on the Web challenges the credi-bility of visual content. In practical Web environments, manipulated media originate from evolving generation pipelines and undergo diverse processing during dissemination, making reliable authen-ticity assessment under unseen manipulations and visual changes a critical challenge. We observe that a standard LoRA-adapted CLIP detector exhibits uneven patch-level decision dependence, concen-trating a disproportionate share of class-specific relevance on a small fraction of visual patches. To improve robustness to varia-tions in such highly dependent evidence, we propose EviStress, a two-stage framework for generalizable face forgery detection with textual explanations. In the first stage, Evidence-Targeted Patch Perturbation (ETPP) estimates patch dependence from global, relational, and semantic cues and assigns positively correlated per-turbation probabilities, explicitly stress-testing highly dependent visual evidence during training. We further introduce Discrimina-tive Variation Preservation (DVP), which constrains the residual between clean and perturbed representations to retain authenticity-discriminative information. In the second stage, Intrinsic Evidence Instruction Tuning (IEIT) transfers the adapted CLIP encoder into LLaVA as its vision tower and performs parameter-efficient mul-timodal instruction tuning, enabling authenticity prediction and textual explanation directly from the learned forgery-sensitive rep-resentation without an external detector. Experiments demonstrate improved cross-dataset and cross-method generalization and cor-ruption robustness, with the multimodal model providing authen-ticity predictions and human-readable explanations.

Media encountered online may originate from previously unseen generation pipelines and further undergo het-erogeneous resizing, compression, transcoding, and re-encoding during dissemination. Consequently, reliable authenticity assess-ment must account not only for unseen manipulation methods, but also for changes in visual evidence induced by online content processing. This work addresses the content-authenticity aspect of Web security, where generalization beyond the training distri-bution is a central requirement for practical face forgery detec-tion [4, 36, 45, 49, 50]. Recent studies increasingly build generalizable detectors upon vision foundation models and vision–language models, particularly CLIP [32]. Its large-scale pretraining provides transferable visual and semantic representations, while subsequent methods improve arXiv:2610.16879v1 [cs.CV] 12 Oct 2026 forgery modeling through parameter-efficient adaptation, local token learning, token reorganization, and pretrained-prior preser-vation [5, 8, 28, 42, 46]. However, improving access to local evidence does not necessarily make the resulting decision robust to changes in that evidence. To examine how a source-trained detector actually uses local visual cues, we quantitatively analyze class-specific patch relevance in a standard LoRA-adapted CLIP detector. As shown in Figure 1(a), its decision relevance is markedly non-uniform: the top 10% patches account for 43.1% of the total class-margin relevance, approximately 4.25 times the mass expected under uniform alloca-tion. Moreover, intervention on the most supportive patches causes greater prediction degradation than removing the same number of randomly selected patches, indicating that these high-relevance regions influence the detector’s authenticity predictions rather than merely being visually salient. These observations motivate a more direct question: instead of forcing the detector to distribute its rel-evance uniformly, can we improve generalization by repeatedly stress-testing the visual evidence on which its current decision depends most strongly? Beyond a binary label, textual explanations provide a human-readable complement to online content authenticity assessment. Multimodal large language models offer a way to generate authen-ticity predictions and explanations within a single framework. Some approaches retain specialized vision detectors for authenticity pre-diction and employ the language model mainly as an interpreter or feature aggregation module [9, 14, 31, 53]. Although this design ben-efits from established detectors, its core detection capability remains external to the MLLM. Other studies directly fine-tune MLLMs with curated forgery image–text data to jointly produce predictions and explanations [13, 38, 44, 54]. However, generic MLLM vision en-coders are primarily pretrained for semantic understanding and are not explicitly optimized for subtle, low-level facial manipulation traces [33, 41]. Consequently, multimodal instruction tuning alone may yield plausible rationales without ensuring that the underly-ing authenticity prediction is built upon a forgery-sensitive visual representation extracted from the input image. To address these issues, we propose EviStress , a two-stage frame-work for generalizable face forgery detection with textual explana-tions. In the first stage, we introduce Evidence-Targeted Patch Perturbation (ETPP) to explicitly stress-test highly dependent vi-sual evidence. ETPP estimates patch dependence through global, relational, and semantic cues and assigns perturbation probabili-ties that are positively correlated with the resulting dependence scores. Instead of uniformly perturbing visual tokens, ETPP prefer-entially applies controlled stochastic modulation to patches with stronger estimated dependence, repeatedly exposing the detector to variations of influential local evidence during training, as illus-trated in Figure 1(c). Since targeted intervention may also introduce class-irrelevant feature variations, we further propose Discrimina-tive Variation Preservation (DVP), which constrains the residual between clean and perturbed representations to retain authenticity-discriminative information. As shown in Figure 1(b), the adapted visual detector exhibits smaller probability drops under the eval-uated post-processing transformations. In the second stage, we present Intrinsic Evidence Instruction Tuning (IEIT) for self-contained explainability. IEIT integrates the adapted CLIP encoder into LLaVA as its vision tower, freezes the visual encoder, and optimizes the multimodal projector and the LLM through LoRA, thereby transferring the learned forgery-sensitive representation to multimodal instruction tuning. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We propose EviStress, a two-stage evidence stress learning framework for generalizable face forgery detection with textual explanations, aimed at supporting Web content au-thenticity assessment. 

• We introduce ETPP , which preferentially stress-tests highly dependent visual evidence during training, together with 

DVP , which preserves authenticity-discriminative informa-tion in the induced representation variation. We further develop IEIT to transfer the adapted visual encoder to an MLLM without relying on an external detector. 

• We quantitatively characterize concentrated patch-level de-cision dependence and show that evidence stress learning improves cross-dataset and cross-method generalization and average robustness under controlled image corruptions. We further assess textual explanation quality through automatic scoring and a blinded human ranking study against the eval-uated open-source MLLMs. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Face Forgery Detection 

Face forgery detection seeks to identify manipulated facial content from subtle visual and temporal inconsistencies. Early approaches mainly exploit low-level artifacts, including color mismatch, abnor-mal head geometry, and blending boundaries [20, 43, 52]. However, such cues are often tied to specific manipulation pipelines and generalize poorly beyond the training distribution. To improve robustness, subsequent studies explore frequency-aware represen-tations [17, 26], pseudo-forgery generation and data augmenta-tion [36, 45], disentangled feature learning [11, 19], and temporal or audio-visual consistency [30, 51]. Despite improved performance, these methods remain vulnerable to manipulation mechanisms and distribution shifts that are absent from the training data. More recently, vision foundation models and vision–language models, particularly CLIP [32], have provided transferable visual and semantic priors for forgery detection. Effort [46] decomposes pretrained representations into orthogonal subspaces, adapting forgery-related components while preserving general visual priors. ForAda [5] introduces lightweight task-specific modules to inject forensic traces into CLIP features. UDD [8] shuffles and mixes patch tokens to reduce positional and content biases. QTFP [42] introduces learnable query tokens to aggregate informative local evidence instead of relying solely on the global token. FIA-USA [28] constructs pseudo-forgeries by simulating transferable facial in-consistencies and up-sampling artifacts. These methods primarily improve evidence extraction, aggregation, or representation adap-tation. In contrast, the robustness of decision-critical local evidence to visual variation is rarely an explicit training objective, leaving detectors vulnerable to key-evidence shifts in unseen distributions.

## 4 Experiments 4.1 Datasets 

For generalization evaluation, we follow prior work [3, 16, 50] and train on the c23 version of FaceForensics++ (FF++) [35]. Celeb-DF-v2 (CDF) [21], DeepFakeDetection (DFD) [34], and the Deep-Fake Detection Challenge (DFDC) [6] are used for cross-dataset evaluation, while DF40 [48] is used for cross-method evaluation following [3, 18]. We report AUC as the primary detection metric at the frame level, with video-level AUC additionally reported for the cross-dataset protocol by averaging frame scores within each video.

## 4.3 Generalizability Evaluation 

Tables 1–3 compare EviStress with recent detectors under cross-dataset and cross-method protocols. Stage 1 achieves an average frame-level AUC of 90.0% and video-level AUC of 94.2% on CDF, DFD, and DFDC, while reaching 96.0% frame-level AUC on the six DF40 manipulation subsets. Stage 2 retains strong generalization after multimodal instruction tuning. Stage 2 moderately decreases from Stage 1 as forgery-sensitive representations are mapped into multimodal space and jointly opti-mized with text generation. ## 5 Conclusion 

We presented EviStress , a two-stage framework for generalizable face forgery detection with textual explanations, motivated by the authenticity challenges of online facial content. In Stage 1, 

ETPP preferentially perturbs highly dependent visual evidence, while DVP preserves authenticity discriminative information in the induced variation. The resulting encoder achieves strong cross-dataset and cross-method generalization and reduced average AUC degradation under the evaluated visual corruptions. In Stage 2, IEIT 

transfers the adapted encoder into an MLLM for joint authenticity prediction and textual explanation without relying on an external detector. A limitation is that generated rationales are not explicitly grounded in patch-level decision evidence, so explanation quality does not necessarily imply causal faithfulness. Future work will explore region–text alignment and intervention-based supervision to strengthen this connection. References
