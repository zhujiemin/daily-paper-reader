## ABSTRACT 

Face swapping and generative editing enable realistic facial manipulation, while online redistribution complicates publisher oversight of circulating face images. Existing approaches establish provenance, detect manipulation, recover embedded watermarks, verify image integrity, or disrupt unauthorized face manipulation. These capabilities do not necessarily establish consistency with a specific publisher-enrolled release, limiting the evidence available for verification and human review. To address this gap, we introduce D OCTOR TRIGGER , a framework for verifying circulating faces against private publisher-held release records. Before publication, the publisher embeds a weak keyed Fourier-phase signal in each face while preserv-ing visual fidelity and stores its spectral reference. Afterward, verification compares the circulating face with that reference and uses key projection to measure retention of the release-specific signal. Therefore, relation and key evidence jointly help publishers identify suspicious copies for review, potentially reducing the circulation of manipulated images. Experimental results across FF++, DFDC, and Celeb-DF show that spectral agreement separates tested face swaps and generative manip-ulations from enrolled releases, while the keyed signal distinguishes published versions from their unreleased originals under the evaluated key-preserving be-nign transformations. D OCTOR TRIGGER complements provenance metadata with release-specific face verification and record-linked evidence for further assessment. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent face and head swapping systems produce realistic identity changes (Wang et al., 2025a; Kang et al., 2025), while compression and resizing also alter images as they circulate through online sharing and reposting. For publishers such as news organizations and public institutions, the question is: does a publicly distributed face still correspond to the specific version that was released? The publisher can retain release-specific evidence before distribution for later verification. To support trust in circulating images, C2PA binds signed provenance assertions to content and supports reconnection through soft bindings (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, 2026). Complementing provenance, recent detectors learn visual-language cues, discrepancies, and large-scale forgery representations (Guo et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Jung et al., 2026). Reference-based detection further compares regional identity and texture with genuine references (Ming et al., 2026). However, these capabilities may not establish whether a circulating face remains consistent with a specific publisher-enrolled release. To address this need, D OCTOR TRIGGER uses private records for release-bound face verification :assessing circulating faces against specific publisher-enrolled releases. First, the publisher applies a weak keyed phase displacement before publication and stores a spectral reference. A publisher-operated verification service, such as a newsroom verification desk, then uses the record to assess face consistency and release-signal retention in user-submitted copies. Finally, the resulting status guides routine handling or further assessment, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The name reflects this workflow: 

Doctor evokes assessment against a retained history followed by triage; Trigger denotes the weak phase signal added before publication while preserving visual fidelity. Conceptually, the reference assesses face consistency; the key helps identify the published version. ## 2 PROBLEM SETUP 

Enrollment and query. The publisher verifies a circulating face against a specific release enrolled before publication. Let I ∈ RNh×Nw ×3 denote the original RGB face crop, with height Nh and width 

Nw in pixels. Enrollment adds a weak keyed signal to produce the released crop I′ and retains a private record R. For asset a and face slot i, this record is Ra,i ; we omit the indices when considering one face. At verification, a request supplies the circulating query J and an identifier resolving its record. Our experiments verify individual crops with known enrollment geometry; the proposed multi-face interface verifies each associated face slot separately. 2Preprint 

Processing channel. Once released, a face may undergo delivery processing. A benign channel 

P specifies recompression, resizing, or another such operation; C contains allowed, calibrated channels. Let R contain the evaluated face re-rendering operators M , such as swapping and diffusion resynthesis (Chen et al., 2020; Rombach et al., 2022). We compare two processing histories: 

Hc : J = P(I′), Hd : J = P(M (I′)) , P ∈ C , M ∈ R . (1) Here Hc represents delivery processing alone, while Hd includes face re-rendering before delivery; neither specifies the editor’s intent. A supported channel must therefore preserve useful separation between these histories. Benign queries calibrate thresholds, while manipulation tests assess detection performance. The evaluated verifier receives the channel and crop geometry as inputs rather than inferring an unknown processing history. 

Verification and human assessment. Verification assesses face consistency and release-signal reten-tion to support publisher decisions. In the proposed workflow, routine cases proceed automatically, while record discrepancies or incomplete verification prompt human assessment. Reviewers use the available scores and record information to examine discrepancies, request missing inputs, and decide whether further verification is needed before acting on a circulating copy. The decision policy formalizes this handoff through verification statuses and a separate review flag.

Preserving visual fidelity. We control the signal with phase strength α, in units of π radians, and blend weight β ∈ [0 , 1] ; c ∈ { R, G, B } indexes color channels. For each channel, enrollment multiplies the active Fourier coefficients by eiαπh s(k), leaving their unblended magnitudes unchanged. The inverse transform is real because hs(−k) = −hs(k). Writing eF for the three edited channel spectra and F−1 for the inverse two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform, we blend the edited image with the original, eI′ = (1 − β)I + βF−1( eF ), where eI′ denotes the unquantized blended image. We clip to [0 , 255] and truncate to uint8. This signal-dependent quantization shapes the encoded release I′; before it, the phase displacement is 

δs(k) = arg {(1 − β) + βe iαπh s(k)}. (3) Here arg returns the principal complex phase in radians. The shared RGB multiplier also displaces luminance, with active magnitudes scaled by p1 − 2β(1 − β)(1 − cos απ ), a second-order change for small α. To enforce visual fidelity, enrollment reduces strength until the encoded release meets PSNR ≥ 35 dB and SSIM ≥ 0.98 against the original. 

Retaining evidence for verification. For later comparison, the template TI stores pre-quantization phase; subtracting the displacement recovers original phase in exact arithmetic. Using wrap( x) = (x + π) mod 2 π − π to map angles to [−π, π ), we obtain, for k ∈ K eff ,

TI (k) = wrap( ϕI (k) + δs(k)) , bϕI (k) = wrap( TI (k) − δs(k)) . (4) The record also stores original phase and amplitude, key, strengths, support, and crop geometry. Explicit original phase avoids reconstruction quantization error. Nine encoded-release projections— one full-ring and eight bandwise—normalize verification scores without reloading release pixels. Before quantization, every active bin receives δs(k) = ±δeff . With β = 0 .15 , α = 0 .05 on FF++/Celeb-DF gives δeff ≃ 0.0235 rad; α = 0 .015 on DFDC gives 0.0071 rad. Independent image-based review. Image-based review adds manipulation cues from the query pixels to the record-based assessment. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Datasets and enrollment. We enroll 7,366 real faces from FF++, DFDC, and Celeb-DF (Rössler et al., 2019; Dolhansky et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020), using native-resolution crops located with SCRFD (Guo et al., 2022) and cross-checked with YuNet or a Haar-cascade face detector. Each face receives a release and private record; verification reads that record without reloading the original. 

Queries and splits. To isolate manipulation effects, each released image is paired with a Sim-Swap (Chen et al., 2020) face swap; both are verified against the same record, yielding 14 ,732 queries. Replacement face identities come from manipulated images in the same dataset and split. 

Metrics and training settings. Face-swap detection AUC compares swaps with paired releases, excluding wrong-key queries; Table 1 also covers StarGAN, UniFace, InSwapper, and SD inpainting. Key-discrimination AUC compares correct and wrong keys, while FPR measures benign rejection. Pass/rejection rates describe the specified criterion, not necessarily joint confirmation. Comparison with peer methods. Each watermarking peer embeds its own mark into the same aligned source crops and undergoes SimSwap with the same replacement identities. Table 2 compares their verification signals. The methods differ in what each verifier reads and what its verdict establishes: • Manifest. C2PA verifies signed assertions about an asset’s provenance (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, 2026), but does not itself compare a circulating face with a publisher’s private enrollment reference. • Embedded marks. Robust message recovery (Tancik et al., 2020; Sander et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025) can succeed after a face swap, leaving little evidence of replacement: WAM and VINE-R yield AUC 0.50 . Fragile marks and decoder mismatch (Neekhara et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2023; He et al., 2026), or landmark displacement (Wu et al., 2026), are more directly sensitive to manipulation, but their effectiveness varies across datasets in Table 2. A recovered mark alone therefore cannot establish consistency with the enrolled face. • Image detectors. Recent detectors learn manipulation cues through discrepancy learning and vision-language representations (Yang et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Jung et al., 2026). Our ResNet-50 control reaches AUC 0.68 on Celeb-DF and 0.76 on DFDC with fixed FF++ weights, illustrating the importance of target-dataset evaluation. In our framework, image-only scores guide review alongside verification against the private release record. • Private face record. Our verifier compares the query with the enrolled release and reads the release-specific keyed displacement under calibrated benign channels. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

DOCTOR TRIGGER enables publishers to assess circulating faces against specific enrolled releases. A private spectral reference and weak keyed signal provide complementary evidence of face consistency and release-signal retention while preserving visual fidelity. Experiments across three datasets support direct verification without a trained model under calibrated processing conditions, making enrollment a basis for subsequent verification. More broadly, D OCTOR TRIGGER connects verification to publisher decision-making: confirmed queries support routine handling, unconfirmed queries identify discrepancies for review, and un-available queries prompt additional information or human assistance. These outcomes could help publishers manage verification at scale and prioritize further assessment. Linking evidence to release records, the framework complements provenance credentials and image-only detection. 9Preprint ETHICS STATEMENT 

Publishers such as news organizations and public institutions could retain enrollment records, while publisher-operated verification services assess copies submitted by users or reviewers. Deployment requires enrollment consent, controlled access to private records, and retention and deletion policies. Verification results could support review of circulating images, but public reports should clearly communicate coverage and uncertainty: consistency with a released version does not establish the truth of the depicted event. ## B RELATED WORK 

Provenance credentials. C2PA Content Credentials bind signed assertions about an asset’s issuer and history to its content (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, 2026). Their validation depends on certificate handling, timestamps, and validator behavior, as studied for C2PA v2.2 (Go-laszewski et al., 2026). Content-carried signals can reconnect circulating copies with provenance information after metadata loss (Simmons & Winograd, 2024). D OCTOR TRIGGER complements this infrastructure with a private reference for face-level comparison against an enrolled release. 

Reference-based face verification. Identity embeddings such as ArcFace assess whether two faces depict the same person (Deng et al., 2019). Reference-based forensics also models person-specific behavior: ID-Reveal learns temporal facial signatures (Cozzolino et al., 2021), while Referee uses a reference video as an audiovisual identity anchor (Boo et al., 2026). More recently, regional identity and texture comparisons address identity-preserving generative manipulations (Ming et al., 2026). These approaches establish an important reference-based alternative to query-only detection. DOCTOR TRIGGER instead associates the reference with a particular released asset and combines face consistency with evidence of its keyed release signal. 

Content authentication. Fragile watermarking detects content changes, while semi-fragile designs tolerate specified processing such as JPEG compression (Fridrich, 1999; Lin & Chang, 2000). Recent methods couple authentication evidence to image content: MetaSeal uses content-dependent cryptographic watermarks for attribution (Zhou et al., 2026), and DeepSignature combines signed content-encoding watermarks with latent-space tamper verification (Graf et al., 2026). Thus, content-dependent verification is an established direction. Our formulation centers on publisher-held spectral records and separate face-relation and release-key evidence under calibrated processing; the reference controls assess what the key adds beyond image and identity similarity. 

Image-only face analysis. Image-only detectors identify manipulation traces through spatial and frequency cues (Afchar et al., 2018; Qian et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2021), temporal consistency, and cross-modal evidence (Haliassos et al., 2021; Zhou & Lim, 2021). Generalization approaches include diffusion-guided disentanglement (Sun et al., 2024), multi-generator discrepancy learning (Yang et al., 2025), and multimodal pretraining (Jung et al., 2026). Related attribution methods identify source generators under closed- or open-world assumptions (Yang et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2026). In a publisher workflow, these query-only signals can support review alongside the release-specific evidence supplied by a publisher-held record. 

Watermarking and provenance. Classical and neural watermarking encode ownership or prove-nance signals before distribution (Cox et al., 1997; Zhu et al., 2018; Tancik et al., 2020; Fernandez et al., 2023). Robust schemes preserve recoverable evidence through editing (Wen et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2025; Sander et al., 2025; Bui et al., 2025). SpecGuard embeds messages through spectral projections in learned features (Alam et al., 2025). Artificial fingerprinting embeds identifying signals in generator training data for later attribution (Yu et al., 2021). Our spectral design relates to phase-domain watermarking (Ó Ruanaidh et al., 1996) and proactive release templates (Asnani et al., 2022). Tree-Ring (Wen et al., 2023) embeds a Fourier-ring pattern in diffusion latents during generation; by comparison, D OCTOR TRIGGER edits an existing image’s phase and retains a private record for later comparison. 17 Preprint 

Proactive deepfake defenses. FaceSigns (Neekhara et al., 2024) and SepMark (Wu et al., 2023) use semi-fragile decoder or tracer outputs. Identity-WM (Zhao et al., 2023), LampMark (Wang et al., 2024), and LIDMark (Wu et al., 2026) connect embedded signals to identity or landmarks. FaceGuard detects manipulation through disagreement between extracted and expected watermarks (Yang et al., 2021). MaLP (Asnani et al., 2023) and FractalForensics (Wang et al., 2025b) localize manipulated regions, while multi-face proactive forensics identifies affected faces and identities (Zhang et al., 2026). FaceProtect derives dynamic watermarks from facial features (Lan et al., 2024). Recover-Mark (An et al., 2026) embeds recoverable face information in surrounding regions; TFPDD (Lai et al., 2026) embeds an identity-aware hash without training. Within this family, D OCTOR TRIGGER 

reads face-relation agreement and release-key retention separately from a private spectral record. Its analytic readings require no fitted model weights, and a learned record model combines their features for joint decisions.

## D EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND REPRODUCIBILITY 

This section specifies enrollment, model training, query generation, and score conventions used by the supplementary experiments. 

Enrollment cohort and crop geometry. From 24 ,527 source frames across FF++, DFDC, and CELEB -DF (Rössler et al., 2019; Dolhansky et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020), faces are located with SCRFD (Guo et al., 2022) and accepted only when YuNet or a Haar-cascade face detector also locates an overlapping face box ( 24 ,209 accepted). We center native-resolution crops on the detected face, using width and height ratios 0.224 and 0.334 relative to the source frame, and save lossless PNGs without padding or resizing. We partition C ELEB -DF by source video to prevent video-level overlap between splits. Each of the 7,366 enrolled real faces receives its own released image and a private NPZ record storing the enrolled phase, original phase and amplitude, keyed displacement, active bins, and the release’s own key projection. Record-based verification reads the query and this stored record; it does not reload the original image. D.1 HYPERPARAMETERS AND TRAINING SETUP 

Enrollment and key settings. Enrollment writes the keyed displacement at α = 0 .05 , so the pre-blend shift is 0.05 π rad, with blend weight β = 0 .15 on the ring κ ∈ [0 .10 , 0.45] of normalized frequency ( α = 0 .015 for the DFDC release configuration). Every face passed at the first rung, so these are also the per-face (αi, β i) stored in the record, where i indexes the enrolled face. The key s is the first 64 bits of SHA-256 over a fixed tag, a master seed, and the sample identifier; it seeds NumPy’s PCG64 generator, whose standard-normal draws us(k) over the Fourier grid give 

h = sign( us(k) − us(−k)) . Bins with k = −k get h = 0 and lie outside Keff . The amplitude floor is Amin = 10 −6 max k AI (k) on the luminance spectrum of the aligned face; it removes at most 2% of a face’s ring bins (median below 0.05% ). The wrong-key comparison draws m=8 keys; feature extraction uses the full ring and 8 radial bands. The MLP bands use normalized Euclidean distance from the center of the shifted array, divided into eight intervals over the active support. For rectangular crops this differs from the cycles-per-pixel radius κ used for enrollment and direct sub-band selection.

## K VERIFICATION LIMITS AND RECORD PRIVACY 

This section examines how regeneration and targeted attacks affect verification, then discusses operating conditions and the protection of private records. K.1 REGENERATION AND ADAPTIVE ATTACKS 

An attacker may seek to make a manipulated face pass verification or cause a genuine release to be rejected. To explore these deployment risks, we examine two routes: diffusion regeneration that may erase release-specific evidence, and targeted perturbations that directly alter verification scores. These tests probe whether the evidence remains useful when processing is deliberately chosen to undermine verification. 

Experimental settings. We evaluate 200 FF++ test pairs in Table 17. SD-Turbo regenerates both the release and its re-rendering at strengths 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4, either over the whole crop or only the face region, without accessing the record. Strength controls how much noise is added before denoising: larger values allow more extensive regeneration and greater departure from the input. These values are diffusion settings, not percentages of pixels changed. Targeted attacks use an L∞

budget of ϵ = 8 /255 on images in [0 , 1] . PGD (Madry et al., 2018) takes 40 steps of ϵ/ 8 against the listed score; NES (Ilyas et al., 2018) uses 24 score queries followed by one signed step. Transfer attacks use a DFDC-trained detector. Because these attacks lower suspiciousness for swaps and raise it for genuine releases, the resulting AUC measures ranking under this two-sided attack, rather than swap-evasion success at a fixed threshold.

Findings. The verification components provide complementary evidence but share vulnerabilities. Attacks against the image-only detector leave spectral comparison informative, whereas attacks against the template can also undermine the record model because it uses related spectral features. Adding a learned head therefore does not by itself protect the system from manipulation of its under-lying evidence. Stronger diffusion regeneration also weakens separation, indicating that substantially regenerated copies require separate channel evaluation before automated verification. For deploy-ment, these findings motivate testing attacks across verification components. Accordingly, processing identified as unsupported should yield unavailable . Successful attacks, however, can satisfy the verification criteria without triggering human review. The component AUCs identify failure modes; evaluating their effect on final verdicts additionally requires testing the complete relation–key policy. 

Future directions. These results motivate exploring evidence distributed across frequency bands and spatial scales, with the aim of preserving discrimination under regeneration and delivery loss. Future directions. To resist evidence transplantation, a complementary direction is to couple the release signal to local facial content and verify consistency across facial regions. Evaluation should measure whether these dependencies improve rejection of transplanted evidence while preserving acceptance under supported processing; their effectiveness remains to be established. K.3 VERIFICATION CONDITIONS AND INTERPRETATION 

Channel evaluation must assess both benign acceptance and manipulation rejection: processing can erase discriminative evidence while releases still pass. Failed comparisons identify discrepancies, not causes; unsupported processing or unresolved geometry requires an unavailable outcome. Evaluation uses individual face crops with known enrollment geometry; scores do not localize facial edits. End-to-end multi-face detection and association, public signed receipts, and C2PA integration remain unevaluated. Human-review evaluation is not reported due to confidentiality obligations. The optional image-only review flag supplies separate evidence under Eq. (11). 

Future directions. Deployment studies could combine channel-support and face-association con-fidence to identify when to return unavailable . End-to-end evaluation should measure verification errors, automated coverage, and reviewer accuracy and workload, including attacks that are confirmed and escape referral. K.4 PRIVACY OF SPECTRAL RECORDS 

Phase-only reconstruction reveals coarse facial structure (Fig. 11), making private records sensitive image-derived data. Access to templates, keys, and scores can therefore expose information beyond the reported verification outcome. 

Future directions. Future work could reduce recoverable facial information while retaining relation and key evidence, jointly evaluating verification performance, reconstruction, and identity leakage. Reporting protocols should authenticate outcomes with minimal disclosure and assess leakage through repeated verification requests. 35
